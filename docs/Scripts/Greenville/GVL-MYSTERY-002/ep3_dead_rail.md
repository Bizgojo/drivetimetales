[START AUDIO DRAMA SCRIPT]

NARRATOR: Thursday, four-fifty in the morning. Third day. At the mile-six cut on the Swamp Rabbit Trail, the old railroad grate stood open, and the wet bootprints on the culvert iron still went one way — down. Corinne Shaw had been under this trail for three days. The warrant to go in after her would arrive at seven o'clock.

NARRATOR: The river had other plans. It had rained hard upstream past midnight, and Grady Polk stood on the bank listening to the Reedy run high and fast in the dark, doing railroad arithmetic.

GRADY POLK: Bore's got a low run near the river end. Granddaddy called it the sump. River comes up two more feet, that run floods to the ceiling.

IVY SHAW: How long?

GRADY POLK: Slack water's done. Two hours, maybe three. Seven o'clock is a funeral, ma'am. We go now or we don't matter.

NARRATOR: Nate Okafor had made his calls at four. By five, forty club riders stood in the fog with their bikes — at the Furman end, at the mile-seven gate, and one rider standing on every grate and vent Grady could name along the corridor. The bell was the code. One ring, position held. Three rings, someone coming up.

NATE OKAFOR: Both trail ends are shut. Every grate has a body on it. Riverbank crew's got lights on the culvert mouth. Nothing comes out of the ground this morning that we don't see.

NARRATOR: Grady took the mile-six grate. Ivy went down behind him and did not ask permission from anyone. Nate stayed up top with the surface and the bells, because somebody had to run the sky.

NARRATOR: The bore was brick gone black, shoulder-wide, low enough that Grady walked bent like a question mark. His light found his grandfather's work everywhere — course lines still true after a century, iron brackets where the steam line had hung. A dead railroad, still holding its breath under the living trail.

NARRATOR: Fifty yards in, sounds began to reach them from above. Thin, strange, carried down through the vent grates. Bicycle bells, one ring at a time, each rider reporting. Somewhere the slap of a jogger who'd slipped past the barriers, footfalls crossing over their heads like weather.

NARRATOR: Ivy touched the wet brick and understood, for the first time all the way down in her body, that her sister had been hearing this for three days. The whole bright world, six feet up. Close enough to hear. Too far to shout through.

NARRATOR: They found Corinne's work before they found Corinne. Chalk marks on the brick at shoulder height, every few yards — small, neat tallies, and beside them arrows scored with a stone. She had mapped her prison in the dark. Counted the days by the joggers overhead. Learned which way the cold air moved and marked where it came from, because moving air meant a mouth somewhere, and a mouth meant out.

GRADY POLK: She's been reading the bore.

IVY SHAW: She reads everything. It's what she's for.

NARRATOR: Then, at the junction where the old steam line forked, they heard the voices.

NARRATOR: A man's voice first — fast, ragged, close to the edge. And then a second voice. Level. Dry. Unhurried, like a woman correcting an invoice.

CORINNE SHAW: Royce. Sit down. The water's not at the bedrolls yet, and pacing burns your lamp.

NARRATOR: Ivy Shaw heard her sister's voice come out of the black, alive, and put her fist against her own mouth to keep the sob inside.

NARRATOR: Around the fork's corner, in a widened chamber where the crew had staged their conduit, Royce Dutton had spent three days coming apart. Foreman, Ridgeline Site Services. Twenty-two years of pours and punch lists, one panicked half-second at a grate, and now this — a lamp running low, a river coming up, and a hostage who would not stop being reasonable at him.

ROYCE DUTTON: You don't get it. There's no version now. Tuesday there was a version — I let you up, you got pictures, fine, I lose the job. Now it's three days. There's no version of me walking out of three days.

CORINNE SHAW: There's the version where I walk out ahead of you and tell them you kept me fed and never raised your hand. That version's still on the table.

[BEAT]

CORINNE SHAW: It comes off the table when the water hits the sump. Your words, Royce. Your tunnel.

NARRATOR: Grady's boot found loose brick. The sound cracked down the bore like a shot, and everything after it happened fast. Dutton's lamp swung to the fork. He grabbed Corinne's arm and hauled her the other way, toward the river run, and slammed the old iron fork-gate shut behind them.

NARRATOR: Grady didn't run. Grady had never run anywhere in his life. He walked to the fork-gate, put his light through the bars, and raised his voice for the first time in twenty-two years of anyone knowing him.

GRADY POLK: Royce Dutton. Listen to the man whose granddaddy built the hole you're standing in.

NARRATOR: Silence, except for water. Then Dutton's voice, stretched thin.

ROYCE DUTTON: I've got her. Stay back. I've got her and I know the way out — the crew ran conduit clean to the river. I'll be gone before your warrant wakes up.

GRADY POLK: You're in the dead rail, son. That fork you took ended in nineteen fifty-one — county plugged it, eight feet of concrete. Your crew's conduit runs the other line. Only mouth left to you is the river culvert, and there's forty bicycles standing on it with the sheriff's number already dialed.

[BEAT]

GRADY POLK: You're a foreman. Read your own site. Water behind you, concrete ahead, lights above. The job's over. All that's left to decide is what kind of man they pull out of this hole.

NARRATOR: In the dark of the dead rail, Corinne Shaw felt the grip on her arm shaking, and she did what she had done for three days. She kept her voice at eight-forty. Steady. Even. One mile at a time.

CORINNE SHAW: Royce. The lights are that way. Walk me toward the lights.

NARRATOR: The lamp was quiet for a long five seconds. Then it turned.

NARRATOR: They came up the main run together as the sky went gray — Corinne in front, Dutton behind her with his hands where Grady's light could see them, Ivy walking backward the whole way because she could not stop looking at her sister. At the river end, the bore opened through a stone culvert onto the bank of the Reedy, and the water stood one foot below the sill and rising, exactly as fast as Grady had figured.

NARRATOR: Corinne Shaw stepped out of the culvert mouth into the first light of Friday's dawn.

NARRATOR: Forty bicycle bells went off at once, up and down the bank, ringing like the trail itself had been holding its breath. Deputies took Royce Dutton at the waterline. He didn't fight. He looked back once at the hole he'd hidden in, and what was on his face wasn't anger. It was relief.

NARRATOR: The reconstruction, when it was all written down, was as small and stupid as these things usually are. Tuesday dawn, Dutton had been at the mile-six grate hauling gear up out of the bore before the trail woke, working fast, working scared, because a woman with a camera had been through his site the evening before and his company still didn't know. Corinne came through at six-eleven, saw the Ridgeline jacket she'd photographed twelve hours earlier, and said so out loud — because noticing was her whole life, and because it never occurred to her, at an eight-forty pace on her own trail, to be afraid of a man in high-vis.

NARRATOR: Half a second of panic. One hand. One open grate. By six-twelve the tunnel was empty and the worst decision of Royce Dutton's life was already behind him, getting bigger every hour he didn't undo it. His crew came to the bore that afternoon, understood what he'd done, and walked off the job without a word to anyone — which the prosecutors would have their own name for. Dutton fed her. Apologized to her. Postponed the decision he couldn't make, for three days, while she quietly learned the bore by its air currents, counted his visits, and kept him level. She was the only person in that hole who never panicked, and she was the one tied to a conduit spool.

NARRATOR: The charges named him and the company both. The bore got a chain-link fence, a state inspection, and its first appearance on a county map in one hundred and four years. The easement review that Ridgeline had tried to tunnel under would now take considerably longer than eighteen months, on account of nobody involved being available.

NARRATOR: On the bank, Ivy finally got both arms around her sister and held on until Corinne patted her shoulder twice, which was Shaw for everything you mean, I mean back.

IVY SHAW: The whole county was dragging the river for you.

CORINNE SHAW: The river never had me. It just kept the door.

NARRATOR: Grady Polk stood a little apart, hat off, looking at the culvert his grandfather had built and he had just used, and Nate Okafor rolled his bike up beside him, for once in his life without a number to offer.

NATE OKAFOR: One hundred and four years off the map. How'd you carry that the whole time and never tell a soul?

GRADY POLK: Wasn't mine to tell. Granddaddy always said the trail you see ain't the only one you're walking.

NARRATOR: Ivy reached into her jacket and brought out the watch. She'd kept it charged and running for three days, because pressing stop had felt like a thing you don't do. She buckled it onto her sister's wrist without a word. The screen glowed in the new light, patient as ever, still mid-run.

CORINNE SHAW: There it is.

NARRATOR: Corinne Shaw stood in the sun at the edge of the Reedy, seventy hours and one mile marker from where the tunnel took her, and pressed stop.
