2. WHY THE SOLUTION SEEMS DIFFICULT: The disappearance is physically impossible. Forty yards of straight concrete, both mouths witnessed, no side passages on any county map. Police default to the easy theories — she walked off, she went in the river — and the only man who suspects otherwise, Grady Polk, doesn't want to say what his family buried under that road a century ago.
3. MINOR PROBLEMS / MIDDLE MOVEMENT: (a) The pace-line finds the shoe and the live watch — impossible scene established. (b) Police push the voluntary-walk-off theory; Ivy Shaw dismantles it — Corinne was bringing her something that night. (c) The watch data eliminates both tunnel mouths: her track simply stops mid-tunnel. (d) Grady Polk deflects Ivy's questions but can't leave it alone — his grandfather's railroad ran more than track through that cut.
4. FINAL SOLUTION (episode-level): The tunnel has a second way out that no map shows. Grady's flashlight finds fresh pry-marks on the rusted grate of a 1922 railway service bore set low in the cut wall — bolts cut new and turned tight again from the inside. The episode's question ("did she just leave?") is answered: no. Someone took her down.
5. WHY THE ENDING IS EARNED: Grady's Greenville & Northern family history is planted in his first scene; the watch and witness evidence methodically eliminate every surface exit, leaving only down; the grate is exactly where a railroad service bore would daylight into the old cut. The listener could get there one beat before Grady's flashlight does.

[START AUDIO DRAMA SCRIPT]

NARRATOR: Corinne Shaw ran the Swamp Rabbit Trail every morning it didn't ice. Out of the Furman lot at five-forty, north toward Travelers Rest, back by seven. Eight minutes and forty seconds a mile, every mile, like a metronome. The dawn regulars set their own watches by her.

NARRATOR: She was thirty-seven and investigated insurance claims for a living. She read the world the way she read a file — looking for the detail that didn't belong. It made her hard to fool and impossible to hurry. It also made her notice things other people were paid to hope nobody noticed.

NARRATOR: On the fourteenth of October, a Tuesday, she clipped her watch on in the parking lot and waved at the six-o'clock pace-line gathering by the rack.

NATE OKAFOR: You're two minutes late, Shaw. The metronome slipped.

CORINNE SHAW: I stopped for a fox at the gate. Bill him, not me.

NARRATOR: Nate Okafor led the club's dawn pace-line, forty riders on a good morning, a dozen on a cold one. Today it was a dozen. He checked his head unit and grinned at her.

NATE OKAFOR: We roll in five. We'll catch you at the tunnel like always.

CORINNE SHAW: You'll catch me because I let you.

[BEAT]

CORINNE SHAW: Hey — tell your riders to stay off the shoulder past mile ten. Somebody's been tearing it up out there. I got pictures yesterday. Ivy sees them tonight, then the county does.

NARRATOR: Nate would remember that line for the rest of his life. At the time, it was just Corinne being Corinne — dry, certain, already running.

NARRATOR: Mile five came up on schedule, the way her miles always did. The beep carried in the cold. Fog stood on the Reedy to her left, right where October kept it, and the trail ran empty and silver ahead of her.

NARRATOR: Mile six sat north of Furman, where the trail ducked under the county road through the old railroad cut. Forty yards of century-old concrete, straight as a rifle barrel.

NARRATOR: Two runners were stretching at the far mouth that morning, waiting on the light. Fog lay on the Reedy River below the grade, thick as wool.

NARRATOR: Corinne went in at six-eleven. Her watch logged the split. Behind her, thirty seconds back, the pace-line came off the last curve in a single quiet file.

NARRATOR: Forty yards of straight tunnel. Twelve riders with lights. And no runner anywhere in it.

NATE OKAFOR: Hold up. Everybody hold. Stop — stop.

NARRATOR: Nate's front wheel came to rest a foot from a running shoe, lying on its side in the middle of the tunnel. Laces still tied. Beside it, face up on the concrete, lay Corinne's watch.

NARRATOR: It was still running. The screen glowed in the dark of the tunnel, patient as ever. Current pace: eight-forty.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: The two runners at the north mouth swore no one had come out. Twelve riders at the south mouth swore no one had doubled back. The fog held the tunnel like a held breath, and Corinne Shaw was gone.

NARRATOR: By nine, the trail was closed from Furman to the mile-seven gate, and the questions had started. By ten, they had already gone wrong.

NARRATOR: The deputies did the work you'd want them to do. They interviewed the two runners at the north mouth — a married couple from Travelers Rest who had been looking straight down the tunnel's throat, waiting for their watches to sync. They walked the cut walls. They flew a drone up and down the corridor from Furman to the mile-eight bridge. A dog team took Corinne's scent from her car, ran it hard into the tunnel — and stopped dead at the shoe. The handler said she'd never seen a track just end like that. Like the ground had been swept.

NARRATOR: At eleven, a woman called in a sighting — a runner in gray crossing at Sulphur Springs, moving fast, looking over her shoulder. For an hour it was hope. Then Nate pulled the timestamp. Corinne's watch had been lying on the tunnel floor, already stationary, nineteen minutes before the sighting. Whoever crossed at Sulphur Springs, it wasn't her. The hope died the way false leads always die — quietly, and taking something with it.

NARRATOR: Ivy Shaw came straight from a night shift at the hospital, still in scrubs, and found a sheriff's deputy explaining to Nate that adults are allowed to walk away from their own lives. Ivy was four years older than her sister and a foot shorter than the deputy, and she did not raise her voice.

IVY SHAW: My sister was coming to my house tonight. She was bringing photographs she wanted the county to see. People planning to disappear don't make plans for Tuesday dinner.

NARRATOR: The deputy suggested the river. Ivy pointed through the tunnel mouth, at the little orange evidence flag standing beside the shoe.

IVY SHAW: The river's forty feet that way, down a bank, through briars. She left her shoe in the middle of a tunnel. You think she swam there?

NARRATOR: The deputy wrote something down and moved off toward his cruiser, and Ivy let him go, because you learn triage in an emergency room. You don't spend your strength on the ones who've already decided. You find the ones still working the problem.

NARRATOR: Nate stood at the south mouth with his head unit in his hand, scrolling the morning's file like it owed him an answer. He spoke carefully, the way he did when the numbers wouldn't behave.

NATE OKAFOR: Ma'am — Ivy. Her watch was broadcasting to the club feed. I have her whole run. She never slowed down. Eight-forty pace into the tunnel, and then the track just... stops. Mid-tunnel. No exit ping at either mouth.

IVY SHAW: Watches lose signal underground.

NATE OKAFOR: They do. Then they reacquire when you come out. Hers never came out. Twenty meters in, end of file.

[BEAT]

NATE OKAFOR: I've led four thousand rides on this trail. Nobody leaves a tunnel through the middle of it.

NARRATOR: The county sent a truck at noon to unlock the service gates for the search teams, and the man driving it was Grady Polk. Trail-maintenance supervisor, twenty-two years. Fifty-eight years old, moved slow, spoke slower. He unlocked the mile-seven gate, looked down the cut at the tunnel for a long moment, and said nothing at all.

NARRATOR: Ivy noticed the looking. She crossed the grass before he could get back in the truck.

IVY SHAW: You're the maintenance man. Polk, right? Tell me about that tunnel.

GRADY POLK: Nineteen twenty-two. Poured for the Greenville and Northern, back when this was rail. My granddaddy drove spikes on this line.

IVY SHAW: I don't need the history. I need to know how a tunnel eats a person.

NARRATOR: Grady looked at her the way men look when they're deciding how much truth costs. He settled on less.

GRADY POLK: Tunnel's a hole with two honest ends, ma'am. It don't eat anybody.

IVY SHAW: Then where is my sister?

[BEAT]

GRADY POLK: Ain't my place to guess.

NARRATOR: Ivy held his eyes for a second longer than was comfortable for either of them.

IVY SHAW: My sister is thirty-seven years old. She notices things for a living. If she saw something on this trail she wasn't supposed to see, Mister Polk — I need the man who knows this corridor better than the county does.

NARRATOR: Grady looked down the cut at the tunnel one more time. Something worked behind his face and lost.

GRADY POLK: I keep the gates locked and the brush cut, ma'am. That's the job.

NARRATOR: He got in the truck. But he didn't drive to the depot, and he didn't go home. Grady Polk spent that afternoon parked where he could see the tunnel, watching deputies walk the riverbank with poles, watching them look everywhere except at the road over their heads.

NARRATOR: Because Grady knew something the county maps didn't show. The railroad hadn't just run track through that cut. His grandfather had talked about it at the kitchen table fifty years ago, tapping the oilcloth with one cracked thumbnail. What's under the road, boy, ain't only the tunnel.

NARRATOR: In the truck, Grady pulled the county's corridor map book off the dash and opened it to the mile-six sheet, the way a man checks a thing he already knows. Trail. Road. Tunnel. River. Clean printed lines and a hundred acres of paper that said nothing else was there. He'd walked this cut for twenty-two years. He'd mowed over the truth every one of them.

NARRATOR: The search wound down at dusk with nothing. No prints on the bank. No break in the briars. Nothing in the water. The deputy told Ivy they'd resume at first light and said the word "voluntary" one more time, and Ivy stood in the emptying parking lot and did not cry, because crying was for after you knew.

NARRATOR: At nine that night, Grady's truck rolled back up the closed trail with its lights off.

NARRATOR: He parked at the mile-six cut and stood a while in the cold, listening. Fog had come off the water again and packed the low ground. Under it, faint, the Reedy worked against its stones — a sound like the river talking through a closed door.

NARRATOR: Grady didn't go into the tunnel. He went past it — down the grade on the north side, along the old cut wall, boots quiet in the wet grass. Fifteen feet from the tunnel mouth, low in the concrete where a century of kudzu and rust had swallowed it, there was a grate. Iron bars, hip-high, older than his father. The mouth of the railroad's 1922 service bore. Sealed since before Grady was born. Bolted shut his entire life.

NARRATOR: He put his flashlight on it.

NARRATOR: The rust was scored bright at four points around the frame. Pry-marks — clean, recent, a day old at most. And the bolts holding the grate were not his grandfather's bolts. They were new steel, torqued down hard, threads still shining.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: Grady Polk stood in the fog with his light on that grate and did the arithmetic he'd been refusing all day. Somebody had opened the old bore within the last day. Somebody had closed it again. And the new bolts had been turned tight from the inside.
