[SFX: trail running footfalls on packed gravel path, evening birdsong, distant Reedy River current]

NARRATOR: Nora Calloway had run the Swamp Rabbit Trail four nights a week for six years. She knew every crack in the pavement, every low branch over the path at mile two, every spot where the Reedy River came close enough to the trail that you could hear it breathing.

NARRATOR: She was not the kind of person who stopped.

NARRATOR: On the last Tuesday of October, she stopped.

[SFX: footsteps halting on gravel, quiet]

NARRATOR: Something was wedged into the support strut of the old Camperdown Bridge. Not litter — the trail crew was meticulous. Something deliberate. A ziplock bag, dark with age, pressed into a crevice just above the waterline.

NARRATOR: Nora stood there for four seconds deciding whether to reach in. Then she reached in.

NARRATOR: The bag contained a single object: a small field notebook, waterproof cover, sealed with a rubber band. She peeled the band off. Opened the cover.

NARRATOR: On the inside page, in faded pencil, was her name.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: Not her name as it appeared on a business card. Her full name — Nora Anne Calloway — in handwriting she had never seen before.

NARRATOR: She looked up and down the trail. Empty. The bridge over the river, the path curving south toward the Augusta Road trailhead, a bench thirty yards back. No one.

NARRATOR: She put the notebook in the back pocket of her running jacket and finished the last mile home at exactly the pace she'd planned.

[SFX: footfalls resuming on gravel, fading]

NARRATOR: That was the last time Nora Calloway felt normal for quite a while.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: She sat at her kitchen table and opened the notebook properly under good light.

NARRATOR: The first page had a hand-drawn map. Not Greenville as it looked now — the Swamp Rabbit Trail as it ran today, marked in blue, and beside it a second trail marked in red, running parallel for about half a mile before disappearing. There was no red trail on any map Nora had ever seen. She worked in landscape architecture. She had studied the city's greenway system for a decade. The red trail did not exist.

NARRATOR: The second page was a sequence of GPS coordinates. Sixteen of them, numbered one through sixteen, written in neat columns.

NARRATOR: The third page had one name: Ruth Elias.

NARRATOR: Below the name, three words: Find it first.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: Nora took out her laptop.

NARRATOR: She typed Ruth Elias into the county historical archive search and got thirty-one results. She filtered by date. The oldest appeared first: a 1987 Carver County Planning Board meeting minute. Ruth Elias, board member, had cast the only dissenting vote on a downtown land acquisition — a sixteen-acre parcel on the west side of the river, zoned for a mixed-use development.

NARRATOR: She searched for the next entry. Another planning board minute, same year. Elias, dissenting again.

NARRATOR: Then a third one, where Ruth Elias had formally requested an environmental impact study before any ground was broken.

NARRATOR: The request was listed as: Denied by majority vote.

NARRATOR: Nora clicked to the next result.

NARRATOR: It was an obituary. Ruth Elias, 44. A construction accident. October 1987. Survived by a daughter and a sister. Services were private.

[MUSIC:OUT]

NARRATOR: Nora read the obituary twice.

NARRATOR: The woman had asked questions about a construction site and was dead three weeks later.

[MUSIC:IN]

NARRATOR: She took the notebook to her desk and opened the mapping software she used for work. She typed in the first coordinate.

[SFX: keyboard typing, brief pause]

NARRATOR: It resolved to a location on the west side of the river. She zoomed in. A construction site — newly fenced, signage going up. Groundbreaking ceremony: tomorrow morning, eight a.m.

NARRATOR: She sat back in her chair.

NARRATOR: The notebook had her name on it. The map showed a trail that didn't exist anymore. The coordinates pointed to a site breaking ground tomorrow. And a woman who had tried to stop a project in 1987 had died before she could.

NARRATOR: Nora was a landscape architect. She read soil the way most people read faces. She understood what cities buried and why.

NARRATOR: She looked at the second coordinate. Then the third. They were all within two miles of each other, all clustered near the river, all connected to a section of the city that had been redeveloped in the late 1980s.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: She thought about calling someone. A friend. Her sister. She thought about calling the non-emergency police line and explaining that she'd found a notebook under a bridge with her name in it and she was concerned about a dead woman from 1987.

NARRATOR: She did not call anyone.

NARRATOR: She went to bed at eleven. She set her alarm for five a.m.

[SFX: alarm clock sound, morning birds]

NARRATOR: The construction site on River Terrace Road was quiet at five-forty-five in the morning. One security guard in a booth at the main entrance, eyes on his phone.

NARRATOR: Nora had dressed for a site visit. Hardhat, reflective vest, field notebook — the props of her profession. She had a business card that said Calloway Land Design and a project in the same development corridor that she could name if anyone asked.

NARRATOR: No one asked.

NARRATOR: She walked the perimeter of the lot with the careful eye of someone who spent her working life reading what ground had been before a developer touched it. The lot was big — maybe four acres cleared and graded. Standard prep. Compaction testing stakes in the southeast corner. Survey markers along the road.

NARRATOR: She walked to the northwest corner.

NARRATOR: She stopped.

[SFX: footsteps stopping on disturbed earth]

NARRATOR: The soil there was a different color than the rest of the lot. Not dramatically — you would miss it if you weren't looking for it, or if you hadn't spent years reading sites. But the fill in that corner was a different composition. Denser. Older. Someone had dug here and covered it, and the covering had never quite matched what surrounded it.

NARRATOR: Not recently. Not last week. Decades ago.

NARRATOR: Nora crouched down and pressed her palm flat against the surface.

NARRATOR: She had stood on a hundred sites like this. She knew what ground felt like after it had been disturbed and left to settle. This ground had been settled for thirty years. Maybe more.

NARRATOR: She stood up and looked at the site plan posted on the fence behind her. The northwest corner was designated for the residential foundation. Four stories. Underground parking.

NARRATOR: If they broke ground tomorrow at eight a.m., whatever was in that corner would be unreachable within six months.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: She took out the notebook and looked at the first coordinate again.

NARRATOR: X marked here. Whatever Ruth Elias had been trying to find thirty-five years ago — it was underneath her feet right now.

[SFX: distant construction equipment engine starting up somewhere across town]

NARRATOR: Nora looked at her watch. It was six-fourteen.

NARRATOR: The groundbreaking was in one hour and forty-six minutes.

NARRATOR: She walked back to her car, got in, and pulled out of the lot. At the first red light, she opened the notebook to the second coordinate.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: She entered it into her phone.

[SFX: GPS navigation chime]

NARRATOR: The second coordinate was half a mile away.

NARRATOR: Nora put the car in drive.
