## GLOSSARY OF KEY TERMS
- **CTR (Click-Through Rate):** The percentage of people who see an ad and click it. Formula: clicks ÷ impressions × 100.
- **CPM (Cost Per Mille):** Cost per 1,000 ad impressions.
- **CPC (Cost Per Click):** Cost each time someone clicks an ad.
- **CPL (Cost Per Lead):** Cost each time someone takes a lead action (e.g., submits name and email).
- **Click objective:** A Meta campaign configured to optimize delivery toward users most likely to click the ad link.
- **Autoplay:** Meta's default behavior of playing video as it scrolls into view, initially muted.
- **In-feed ad:** An ad appearing in the main Facebook or Instagram scrollable feed among organic posts.
- **Story ad:** A full-screen vertical ad appearing between organic Stories.
- **Kinetic text:** On-screen text that moves, fades, or animates, as opposed to static text cards.
- **Caption:** Verbatim audio transcript displayed as subtitles synchronized with speech.
- **Text card:** A full-screen or overlay frame of authored text, not verbatim audio transcript.
- **Hook:** The first seconds of an ad, designed to stop scrolling and establish interest.
- **Trial-gate / name-and-email gate:** A landing page requiring personal information before granting access.

## 1.1 How Audio Subscription Services Advertise on Meta

### What Was Attempted and Why Evidence Is Limited

This research question proved the hardest to answer with documented evidence. The primary reason is that Meta's Ad Library (facebook.com/ads/library), the canonical public source for seeing exactly what ads are running, requires authenticated login to view full creative details—it returned a 403 error to unauthenticated fetches. No published case studies from Pocket FM, Dreame, Kuku FM, Serial Box, Realm, or Wattpad about their Meta campaigns were accessible at the URLs attempted. **Audible (Amazon):** Audible is the dominant audiobook subscription service in the US, with a free trial prominently featuring in its acquisition marketing. The service's long-standing offer has been "Listen free for 30 days, then [price]/month." Whether Audible currently runs video or static image creative on Meta, and what its first-frame hook strategy is, cannot be documented from what was retrieved during this session.

**Pocket FM:** Pocket FM is an audio fiction platform (audio series/drama format, not audiobooks) that has reported surpassing 100 million users. The service is known to have run aggressive social media advertising to grow its US user base. Pocket FM's ads have been described in general press coverage as text-heavy, story-excerpt formats that tease cliffhangers—consistent with the general category behavior of "show the reader a compelling excerpt, make them want more." However, the specific Meta creative format (video vs. static, text card structure, hook strategy) could not be confirmed from accessible sources during this session.

**Dreame, Kuku FM, Wattpad, Serial Box, Realm, Storytel:** These services are known competitors but no accessible published documentation of their specific Meta ad creative was found. Storytel is a Scandinavian audiobook service operating internationally; its Meta campaigns in the US have not been publicly documented in accessible sources as of this writing.

### What Can Be Inferred (Not Evidence)

The genre of audio fiction apps (Pocket FM, Dreame, and similar) is known in general press to favor text-heavy, hook-first creative that leads with a story fragment—because text can be read silently and thus works with muted autoplay. This is inference from category behavior and general press coverage, not a documented finding with citable source and date.

**Confidence Level:** INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE for specific creative details of named competitors on Meta.

## 1.2 Hook and Text Treatment for Silent-Autoplay Feed Video

### The Muted Autoplay Reality

Meta's own research, widely cited by industry sources, found that **80% of people reacted negatively to videos auto-playing with sound**, which led Meta to change the default so videos in-feed play muted until the viewer taps to unmute. As a result, the majority of Facebook video ads are watched without sound. This finding is reported in AdEspresso's Facebook Video Ads Guide (adespresso.com, updated through 2020–2021; original source cited as Meta's own research announcement).

This has a direct implication: an ad whose entire value proposition depends on audio is structurally disadvantaged in in-feed placement. Any audio drama subscription ad must communicate its hook through on-screen visuals and text alone.

### Caption vs. Text Card

**Video ads with captions (verbatim audio transcript synchronized with speech) are watched 12% longer** than non-captioned video ads. This is Meta's own finding, published in their research about video ad features and cited by AdEspresso (adespresso.com, 2020–2021). The specific study date is not provided in the secondary source.

Note: This 12% longer watch-time figure applies to captions (verbatim transcript), not to authored text cards. The Bell campaign uses silent text cards rather than captions synchronized to speech—because there is no speech track to caption. This is a different format. No direct study comparing authored silent text cards to captioned speech was found.

### Timing of Value Delivery

From Meta's own research as cited by AdEspresso (adespresso.com):
- **The first 3 seconds of a video ad deliver 47% of the brand value** for that placement.
- **The first 10 seconds deliver 74% of the brand value.**
- **Brand mentions in the first 3 seconds are positively correlated with conversion lift.**
- **People have significantly stronger recall for ads that get the point across in the first 5 seconds.**

These figures come from Meta's internal research and are described in their published "Value of Video" and "Making Creative Impact" research briefs; the secondary citation is AdEspresso's guide. The underlying primary sources are no longer directly accessible at their original URLs but are consistently cited across multiple industry sources.

### Words Per Card: No Specific Evidence Found

No published study was found that documents an optimal word count per text card for silent, muted, in-feed video ads. The general principle that short, punchy text outperforms dense text in mobile-first formats is widely stated but without controlled test documentation in the accessible sources retrieved.

### Kinetic vs. Static Text: No Specific Evidence Found

No study comparing kinetic/animated text (text that moves or fades into frame) versus static text cards in terms of CTR or conversion for in-feed silent video was found in accessible sources. This is a gap.

**Confidence Level:** HIGH for the muted autoplay reality and the timing findings (Meta's own research). LOW for words-per-card and kinetic vs. static text comparisons (no evidence found).

**Implication for Bell campaign:** The 8-second, three-card text format is structurally appropriate for muted autoplay. The first card must establish the hook before the 3-second mark, because that's when nearly half the ad's brand value is delivered. Put the place name ("Greenville") and genre signal in the first card, not the third.

## 1.3 Optimal Ad Length for Click Objective in 2026

### Meta's Official Guidance

Meta (via its Help Center and Business resources) recommends keeping video ads **under 15 seconds**, a guideline consistently cited in industry sources including Hootsuite's "How to Advertise on Facebook in 2026" (blog.hootsuite.com, 2026). Facebook Stories ads longer than 15 seconds are automatically split into multiple cards; in-stream ads play the first 15 seconds before the viewer is given a choice to keep watching or return. The 15-second threshold appears in multiple product placements as a natural breakpoint.

Technical limits: In-feed video ads can be up to 241 minutes technically, but this is the outer boundary, not a recommendation.

### Does the "Under 15 Seconds" Rule Still Hold in 2026?

The Hootsuite guide (2026) repeats the under-15-second recommendation without qualification or counter-evidence. No accessible independent research was found that tested this specifically for click-objective campaigns in 2025–2026 and reached a contradicting conclusion. The rule appears unchanged in current guidance.

### The Bell Campaign's 8-Second Format

The Bell campaign's planned 8-second length falls well within the recommended range. No evidence was found suggesting that 8-second videos underperform compared to 12–14-second videos for click objectives in the entertainment or subscription category. This specific comparison was not found tested in accessible sources.

**Confidence Level:** HIGH that "under 15 seconds" remains supported guidance. INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE for whether 8 seconds specifically is better or worse than 12–14 seconds for this ad type.

**Implication for Bell campaign:** 8 seconds is appropriate. The constraint is not length but what happens in those 8 seconds—specifically whether the first-card hook, delivered before the 3-second mark, is strong enough.

## 1.4 Static Image vs. Short Video for Fiction/Narrative Category

### General Cross-Category Evidence

The most directly relevant survey is from Databox (databox.com, "Video Ads vs. Image Ads: Which Drive More Engagement on Facebook?"), which surveyed 95 marketers:
- **67.55% said video content drives more ad clicks on Facebook**
- **26.47% said images perform better; 5.88% said no difference**
- **63.24% use video more often** in their Facebook Ads

The same survey included an important caveat: at least one respondent (Kimberly of Frugal Livin) reported that "image ads have a better CTR and CPC than video ads" in their experience, and they "stopped using video ads for clicks and conversions" as a result. The Databox survey does not provide industry breakdown, so this data covers multiple categories.

### Meta's Own Research

Meta's internal research, cited by AdEspresso (adespresso.com), states that:
- **People look at video content 5x longer than static content** on Facebook.
- **69% of marketers say video ads outperform image and plain text ads** on Facebook.

An example from the agency ClearPivot: **20–30% higher conversion rates with video** in Facebook ads. This is a single agency's observation across an unspecified client mix and does not isolate fiction or subscription categories.

### Fiction/Narrative Category: No Specific Evidence

No published split test or category-specific study comparing video versus static image specifically for audio fiction, audiobooks, or narrative fiction subscription services was found. This is a genuine gap. The general marketing evidence favors video, but whether it holds in this specific category—where the product is audio and the ad is silent—is untested in documented, accessible sources.

The theoretical case for video in this category: slow-push motion (as used in the Bell campaign) creates atmosphere and signals "cinematic" quality that static images cannot. The theoretical case for a strong static image: a single, high-quality atmospheric image with bold text might communicate the genre and hook as effectively as 8 seconds of slow motion, at lower cost and with no loading penalty. Neither position is supported by fiction-specific data.

**Confidence Level:** MEDIUM for the general preference for video (survey-based, cross-category). INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE for fiction/narrative/audio drama specifically.

**Implication for Bell campaign:** The general evidence favors video over static image for click campaigns. The slow-push motion is consistent with the atmospheric storytelling category. However, a static image A/B test at launch would be valuable because no fiction-specific data exists to rule it out.

## 1.5 What Converts for Narrative Fiction

### Finding

No published A/B tests, case studies, or documented conversion evidence was found specifically for fiction subscription advertisers using Meta ads. Specific approaches—cliffhanger hooks, character voice, premise statements, mystery questions—are widely recommended by content marketing practitioners, but this recommendation class is not supported by controlled test documentation in accessible sources.

The closest adjacent category with some documented evidence is general subscription services and SaaS trials, but that evidence addresses offer framing (see 1.6) rather than creative hook type.

In general content marketing literature, the "open loop" / cliffhanger technique—presenting a story fragment that creates unresolved tension and cannot be resolved without clicking through—is the most commonly recommended approach for driving click-through on fiction content. But "most commonly recommended" is not the same as "documented to outperform alternatives in a controlled test."

**Confidence Level:** INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE for documented conversion lift from any specific hook type in fiction subscription ads.

**Implication for Bell campaign:** The cliffhanger approach ("something happened in Greenville" → mystery established → "listen free") is consistent with the general recommendation. No evidence contradicts it. But the only way to get documented evidence specific to Endless Tales is to run tests.

## 1.6 Trial-Offer Framing

### Finding

No specific, published A/B test comparing "listen free," "7-day free trial," "try free for 7 days," "first episode free," or similar trial offer framings for audio or media subscriptions on Meta was found in accessible sources.

What is known from the broader subscription industry:

- **Audible's long-standing framing** has been "Listen free for 30 days." This positions the trial as experiential ("listen") rather than transactional ("get a free month"), which is notable for an audio product. This is an observation, not a tested finding.

- **Spotify** has used "Try Premium free for 3 months" and "Start free." Both are common; Spotify has not publicly disclosed which outperforms the other.

- General landing-page and direct-response literature suggests that specificity in trial offers (naming the number of days or episodes) outperforms vague offers ("try it free"), because specificity reduces perceived risk and increases cognitive commitment. This general principle is supported by direct response practitioners but no specific audio subscription test was found.

- The phrase "no credit card required" has been tested in SaaS contexts and consistently found to increase trial signup rates versus offers that require a credit card upfront. This is well-established in SaaS but not documented specifically for audio subscription on Meta.

- The Bell campaign's current framing ("Listen free" plus the 7-day free trial and no-credit-card benefit on the landing page) combines an experiential CTA with a low-friction offer. Whether putting "no credit card" in the ad itself (rather than on the landing page) would increase CTR or lower CPL is untested in this context.

**Confidence Level:** LOW for direct evidence on this specific copy choice. MEDIUM for the general principle that "no credit card required" reduces friction.

**Implication for Bell campaign:** "Listen free" is consistent with Audible's long-standing experiential framing for audio. The question of whether to add "no credit card" to the ad creative itself (vs. landing page only) should be treated as a testable hypothesis, not a settled decision.

## 1.7 Advertising AI-Generated Content

### Regulatory Landscape as of 2026

**Meta's policy (as of mid-2026):** Meta requires disclosure of AI-generated content specifically in **election and political ads**, not for entertainment or commercial ads in general. Meta added a disclosure requirement for realistic synthetic media in political advertising in 2023–2024, but this does not apply to entertainment subscription ads. Note: This policy may have evolved. The exact Meta policy page on AI-generated content could not be accessed (returned an error), and this should be verified directly in Meta's current Ad Standards before launch.

**FTC guidance:** The FTC has issued guidance requiring disclosure of material connections in advertising (endorsements, etc.), and has discussed AI disclosure in the context of misleading advertising. However, the specific FTC guidance pages attempted during this research returned 404 errors, and the current regulatory requirement for disclosing AI-generated audio entertainment in a commercial ad is not definitively established from what was retrieved. The FTC's position as of late 2023 is that AI can be used in advertising but cannot be used to create deceptive material connections or false impressions. Whether an undisclosed AI-generated audio drama constitutes a deceptive practice has not, to this researcher's knowledge, been litigated or formally adjudicated.

**Consumer response to AI disclosure:** No published, methodologically rigorous study was found documenting the effect on CTR or conversion when "AI-generated" is disclosed in ad copy vs. omitted. Consumer research on AI in content is mixed: some studies (in adjacent contexts like AI-generated music and AI-written articles) suggest disclosure can reduce perceived quality or authenticity. However, these studies are not specific to audio drama, to Meta ad copy, or to the conversion action of signing up for a free trial. The AI content disclosure–conversion relationship is undocumented in this specific context.

**What is known without a controlled test:** In 2024–2026, AI-generated content became widespread. Consumer awareness that content may be AI-generated has increased. Whether awareness is the same as negative reaction depends heavily on category and framing. An audio drama listener who enjoys the story may not care about the production method. An early adopter skeptical of AI may react negatively to disclosure or to discovery of AI origin.

**The specific risk the campaign faces:** If Endless Tales does not disclose AI generation in ad copy, and a significant portion of its audience later discovers the content is AI-generated, word-of-mouth or review backlash could affect retention. This is a brand risk, not a campaign-launch risk. The ad itself, under current Meta policy, does not appear to require AI disclosure for entertainment content.

**Confidence Level:** MEDIUM for the regulatory picture (Meta's political-ad disclosure requirement is established; general entertainment disclosure requirement is not). INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE for the effect of AI disclosure on CTR or conversion in this specific context.

**Implication for Bell campaign:** No regulatory mandate has been found requiring AI disclosure in ad copy for entertainment subscription services at this time (verify against current Meta Ad Standards before launch). The conversion risk of voluntary disclosure is unknown. The brand reputation risk of non-disclosure followed by discovery is real but not quantified. This is a business decision, not a research finding.

# SECTION 2 — BELL CAMPAIGN RECOMMENDATION

The following recommendations are derived from the evidence above. Each cites the specific finding that supports it. Where evidence is absent, this is labeled as a **judgment call**.

---

### 2.1 Format: Video

**Recommendation:** Keep video. Do not substitute a static image as the primary creative.

**Evidence basis:** The general evidence for video over static image is consistent across multiple sources. 67.55% of 95 surveyed marketers report video drives more clicks (Databox). Meta's research shows people look at video 5x longer than static content (AdEspresso citing Meta). 69% of marketers report video outperforms images and text (AdEspresso citing Databox). Agency ClearPivot observed 20–30% higher conversion rates with video.

**Caveat:** No fiction-specific or audio drama-specific comparison was found. The evidence is cross-category. A static image test is recommended as a parallel variant (see Section 3).

---

### 2.2 Length: 8 Seconds — Appropriate, Keep It

**Recommendation:** 8 seconds is consistent with guidance. Do not extend to 15 seconds.

**Evidence basis:** Meta and its partners consistently recommend video ads under 15 seconds (Hootsuite 2026, citing Meta). The 8-second length is within best-practice range. No evidence was found that 8 seconds underperforms versus 12–14 seconds for click objectives.

---

### 2.3 Number of Text Cards: Three Cards Is Reasonable — With One Structural Constraint

**Recommendation:** Retain three text cards. But restructure so the **hook is established by second 2–3, not by second 8**.

**Evidence basis:** Meta's research (via AdEspresso) documents that the first 3 seconds deliver 47% of brand value, and that brand mentions in the first 3 seconds correlate with conversion lift. With three cards across 8 seconds, each card receives approximately 2.5–3 seconds. The **first card must carry the hook and the brand signal**—not a setup card that merely establishes atmosphere. The Bell campaign's described structure ("three text cards telling a story hook, ending in 'Greenville. Listen free.'") suggests the brand/offer appears in the third card, at seconds 6–8. This is structurally late based on Meta's research.

**Specific recommendation:** Move the name "Endless Tales" or "Greenville" (the brand signal) to the first card, even if briefly. Reserve the mystery/cliffhanger for cards two and three.

---

### 2.4 Words Per Card: Judgment Call (No Evidence Found)

**Recommendation (judgment call, not evidence-based):** Keep each card to 5–8 words maximum. The reasoning is that in 2–3 seconds of display time, a viewer reading at average pace (~200–250 words per minute) can process approximately 8–12 words, but shorter text registers faster and is more likely to be fully absorbed before the card changes. This is informed by general readability principles, not a controlled test on Meta.

---

### 2.5 Hook Approach: Mystery Question / Cliffhanger

**Recommendation:** The mystery/cliffhanger structure ("something happened in Greenville") is the most defensible creative approach for fiction subscription acquisition.

**Evidence basis:** This is partially a judgment call, but it is consistent with the general principle that "open loops" create curiosity and drive click behavior. No A/B test from fiction subscription advertisers was found (as documented in 1.5). The alternative—a premise statement ("A true-crime audio drama set in Greenville, SC")—is more informational but less emotionally engaging. Neither has been tested head-to-head for this product type in documented literature.

**Supporting logic:** The product is an audio experience, not a visual one. The ad cannot demonstrate the product (you cannot hear it silently). What it can demonstrate is the desire to hear more. The cliffhanger approach creates that desire more directly than a premise statement.

---

### 2.6 Trial-Offer Language: "Listen Free" — Appropriate, Consider Testing "No Credit Card Required" in Ad Copy

**Recommendation:** "Listen free" is appropriate and consistent with established audio subscription framing (Audible's long-running "listen free" offer). Consider testing a variant that adds "No credit card" to the ad creative itself rather than holding that benefit for the landing page.

**Evidence basis:** The "no credit card required" message is documented in SaaS contexts to reduce friction (general literature, no audio-specific study found). Currently the campaign places this benefit on the landing page. Moving it into the ad would surface the lowest-friction element of the offer earlier in the click journey. This should be treated as a hypothesis to test rather than a settled recommendation.

---

### 2.7 AI-Generated Content Disclosure: No Mandate Found — Business Decision

**Recommendation:** There is no evidence of a current regulatory mandate requiring disclosure of AI-generated audio drama content in Meta ad copy for entertainment subscriptions. The decision whether to disclose is a brand and business decision, not a compliance decision based on this research.

**Evidence basis:** Meta's AI disclosure requirements are limited to political/election advertising as of this research. FTC guidance on AI in advertising is developing but no final rule specifically requiring disclosure for entertainment content was confirmed. The effect of disclosure or non-disclosure on conversion is undocumented.

**Flag:** This should be re-verified against current Meta Ad Standards and FTC guidelines immediately before campaign launch, because this regulatory area is actively evolving.

# SECTION 3 — OPEN QUESTIONS

These are things this research pass could not answer that would materially affect campaign decisions.

---

### 3.1 What Exact Creative Do Pocket FM, Audible, Dreame, and Kuku FM Run Today on Meta?

**Why it matters:** These are the most direct competitors. Knowing exactly what their first-frame hook is, what format they use (video vs. static), and what text treatment they use would allow the Bell campaign to differentiate deliberately or match proven patterns.

**How to get the answer:** Access the Meta Ad Library (facebook.com/ads/library) while logged in with any Facebook account. Search "Pocket FM," "Kuku FM," "Audible," "Dreame," and "audiobook" with the US filter and Active ads. This is a one-hour task that yields direct creative comparisons. This should be done before launch.

---

### 3.2 Do Text-Card Silent Videos Outperform Strong Static Images for Audio Drama Specifically?

**Why it matters:** All evidence retrieved was cross-category. Audio drama is unusual: the product is invisible, the ad is silent, and the visual must do the selling alone. Static images with compelling text may perform as well as or better than the slow-push video for this specific product type.

**How to get the answer:** Run a split test at launch. A/B test the 8-second video against a single-image static ad with identical copy. Measure CTR and CPL at equivalent spend. This is answerable within 2–4 weeks of launch.

---

### 3.3 How Many Words Per Card and What Card Duration Maximizes Dwell and Click?

**Why it matters:** The specific text density and pace of card transitions could significantly affect whether viewers absorb the hook before scrolling past.

**How to get the answer:** Test 2-second vs. 3-second vs. 4-second card holds, and short (4–5 words) vs. medium (8–10 words) text per card. This requires creative variants and at least 2–4 weeks of testing. Alternatively, consult a motion designer with Meta video ad experience for heuristics, acknowledging they are practitioner knowledge, not controlled data.

---

### 3.4 Does Moving "No Credit Card Required" Into the Ad Creative Reduce CPL?

**Why it matters:** If this message reduces friction, surfacing it in the ad copy rather than the landing page could increase click rate and/or reduce bounce on the landing page.

**How to get the answer:** Run an A/B test with ad variant A ("Listen free → Greenville") versus variant B ("Listen free · No credit card"). Measure CPL, not just CTR, because a lower-friction ad might increase clicks that don't convert.

---

### 3.5 What Does AI Disclosure Do to CTR and CPL for This Product?

**Why it matters:** If there is a meaningful negative effect on conversion, the campaign should know before scaling. If there is no effect—or a positive one (transparency as a differentiator)—that's also useful.

**How to get the answer:** Run an A/B test with an ad variant that includes a line such as "AI-crafted audio drama" vs. the current copy that omits AI origin. Measure CTR and CPL. This is testable in 2–4 weeks at moderate spend.

---

### 3.6 What Is the Appropriate Landing Page Conversion Rate Baseline for This Offer?

**Why it matters:** The campaign is optimized for clicks to a landing page where a name-and-email gate follows an audio promo. A low click-to-signup conversion rate could mean the ad is driving the wrong audience, the audio promo is not compelling, or the gate is too early in the audio. Understanding the expected baseline helps interpret early results.

**How to get the answer:** Run a small-scale test ($500–$1,000 budget) before committing to full launch budget. Measure click-to-lead rate on the landing page and use it to set CPL benchmarks for the full campaign.
