# PART 1 — PRIMARY MISSION

The purpose of an Endless Tales story is to create listener desire for the next episode.

Not to educate. Not to inspire. Not to deliver a message. To make the listener want more.

Education, inspiration, and meaning can be present — and often make stories better. But they are outputs, not inputs. They emerge from well-crafted stories. They are never the starting point.

**The Primary Test.** At the end of every non-finale episode, ask: **Would a listener immediately start the next episode?** If the answer is no — or even "probably" — the story has failed its primary objective.

**The Meal Rule.** The story is the meal. The lesson is seasoning. Stories with no theme are forgettable. Stories that exist to deliver a theme are lectures. Endless Tales produces neither.

---

# PART 2 — ENTERTAINMENT FIRST RULE

The primary purpose of an Endless Tales story is to **entertain**. Listeners come for suspense, curiosity, emotion, mystery, wonder, humor, fear, connection, and the desire to know what happens next.

- Never interrupt a story to teach a lesson.
- Never have the narrator explain the meaning of the story.
- Never include speeches whose primary purpose is to educate, persuade, moralize, lecture, or preach.
- Never allow a character to become the author's mouthpiece.

If a story contains a lesson, insight, emotional truth, or moral theme, it must emerge naturally through character choices, consequences, conflict, sacrifice, failure, success, and events. **The listener should discover meaning. The writer should not explain meaning.** Questions are usually more powerful than answers.

Stories must never feel like: sermons, lectures, essays, political arguments, social commentary pieces, or self-help content disguised as fiction.

**The priority: Story first. Theme second. Lesson last.**

---

# PART 3 — THE LISTENER PROMISE

**Every story delivers at least one of:**
- **A mystery worth solving** — an unanswered question that drives the listener forward
- **A capable person facing a difficult problem** — skill tested beyond capability; the gap is where the listener leans in
- **A meaningful relationship under pressure** — a connection that must survive conflict
- **A hidden truth waiting to be uncovered** — something that, when revealed, changes everything that came before
- **A world the listener wants to spend more time in** — a setting compelling enough that remaining in it matters as much as the plot

**Every story avoids all of:**
- **Boredom** — empty stretches with no forward momentum
- **Confusion** — stories that require notes or replays; commuters cannot do this
- **Lectures** — the listener is not a student
- **Pointless complexity** — complexity is permitted; pointless complexity is not
- **Meaningless endings** — every ending must deliver catharsis, revelation, or transformation

**The core commitment: the listener should always feel their time was respected.**

---

# PART 4 — COMMUTER REALITY RULE

The primary audience: commuters in cars, drivers on long routes, workers with ambient listening time, exercisers with divided attention. They may briefly lose focus, miss 30 seconds, and reset attention at any moment.

**The standard: stories must remain understandable if the listener misses 30 seconds of audio.** This is a production requirement, not a suggestion.

**Requirements:**
1. **Regular re-orientation.** After every scene break, the narrator or dialogue establishes: who we are with, where we are, when (if relevant), and what just happened (one sentence). Never assume continuity — the listener may have been interrupted.
2. **Clear character identification.** Character names stated at least once after every scene break or significant time jump. Never rely on character voice alone.
   - GOOD: `NARRATOR: Deputy Hale returned to the canyon just after dawn.`
   - BAD: `NARRATOR: He returned to the canyon.` (if the listener missed the setup, they don't know who "he" is)
3. **Clear stakes.** What is at risk in this scene? The listener must know within 30 seconds, even tuning in mid-scene.
4. **Clear scene transitions.** Signal moves to a new place or time with an anchor SFX and/or a narrator cue (anchor SFX rules in Spec v1.5: 3–6 bold effects per story, gaps only).

**The Re-Orientation Checklist** (Vega's gate before any audio reaches Ready For Review):
- [ ] Every scene break includes character re-identification
- [ ] Stakes are stated or demonstrated in the first 30 seconds of any scene
- [ ] Time/place transitions are marked with an anchor SFX or narrator cue
- [ ] No critical plot information appears in a single throwaway line
- [ ] A listener who missed one exchange could still follow the story

# PART 5 — THE SEVEN RETENTION PILLARS

Every story is evaluated against seven retention properties. A 5–7 episode series should hit all seven before the finale. A standalone must hit at least four.

1. **Curiosity** — an active, specific unanswered question at every moment. *Who left those tracks? What is she hiding? Why did he go back?*
2. **Competence** — capable protagonists facing problems that exceed their capability. The gap is where tension lives.
3. **Escalation** — each episode ends in a state of higher stakes than it began. Not different — higher.
4. **Emotional Investment** — the listener must care what happens to at least one character. Non-negotiable. Plot tension without character investment is a puzzle; stories are not puzzles.
5. **Cliffhangers** — non-finale episodes end with an unresolved hook of sufficient force that the next episode becomes difficult to resist (Part 7).
6. **Consistent Quality** — one weak episode damages trust in all subsequent episodes. Listeners forgive a single miss; they do not return after two.
7. **Listener Belonging** — over time the best series make a listener feel they belong to a world. This is the franchise condition. It cannot be manufactured; it is earned across episodes.

---

# PART 6 — SERIES-FIRST FRAMEWORK

Endless Tales is a series platform first. Standalones serve four purposes: discovery, onboarding, testing, and variety — and every standalone hands the listener to a series via Belle's bridge line (Spec v1.5). A finished standalone is a complete experience; a finished series episode is incomplete by design. **Incompleteness is the engine of return.**

**Preferred series lengths:**
| Length | Use case |
|---|---|
| 3 episodes | Tight genre premise, high concept, minimal setup — published complete (Series Release Rule, Spec v1.5) |
| 5 episodes | Standard series unit — most protagonists and worlds fit here |
| 7 episodes | Complex multi-thread stories, deeper world-building, ensembles |
| 13 episodes | Full season format — major franchise-candidate series only |

Series shorter than 3 or longer than 13 require explicit Marc approval. Series episodes default to 12–18 minutes (Spec v1.5).

**Series Architecture Requirements — designed before Episode 1 is written:**
1. **The Central Question** — what question does this series answer? (One sentence.)
2. **The Protagonist Arc** — how the protagonist changes from Episode 1 to finale.
3. **The Escalation Map** — how stakes increase episode to episode.
4. **The Finale Resolution** — the emotional type of the ending (justice, sacrifice, transformation, revelation…).
5. **The Cliffhanger Chain** — for each non-finale episode, one sentence describing the specific hook. **Required before Episode 1 enters production.** A series without a defined cliffhanger chain is not ready to produce.

The Series Release Rule (Episode 1 never publishes without Episode 2 live; 3-episode series publish complete) is owned by Spec v1.5 and is non-negotiable.

# PART 7 — CLIFFHANGER RULE

Non-finale episodes must end with one of five types:
- **Revelation** — new information that reframes everything just heard
- **Danger** — a character in immediate threat with no resolution
- **Betrayal** — an alliance broken, a trust violated, an assumption destroyed
- **Discovery** — something uncovered that opens a new and larger problem
- **Decision** — a character forced to an irreversible choice the listener cannot predict

**Weak cliffhangers (not acceptable):**
| Weak pattern | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "Things are going to get worse" | Vague. No specific hook. |
| Escaping one danger into another of the same type | Repetitive peril erodes trust |
| Cliffhanger resolved in the first 60 seconds of the next episode | Signals the writer doesn't believe in it |
| A new character appears and says something ominous | Misdirection without substance |
| "Little did she know..." | Telegraphed dread, not a cliffhanger |
| Weather/traffic/mechanical failure as stakes | Circumstantial peril, not story peril |

**The Fake Cliffhanger Test:** a real cliffhanger can be written as a specific question the listener cannot resolve without the next episode.
- REAL: *"Who was the second person in the canyon that night?"*
- FAKE: *"What will happen to Deputy Hale?"* (applies to every episode)
- REAL: *"Did Cross arrange the sheriff's death — and does Lucy know?"*
- FAKE: *"The danger was only beginning."*

# PART 8 — NEDS: NEXT EPISODE DESIRE SCORE (the brief gate)

Every episode brief receives a NEDS score **before production is finalized** — at the brief stage, not after audio exists. A score below 7 requires revision before production. NEDS protects the production budget; the 30-point grading rubric (MASTER_BIBLE v3.1 §4) protects the catalog after listening. **Both apply. They measure different moments and neither replaces the other.**

**Scoring rubric (max 10):**
| Category | Range | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| **Curiosity** | 0–3 | 3: multiple active open loops · 2: one strong loop · 1: weak/minor question · 0: nothing unresolved |
| **Stakes** | 0–2 | 2: personal, consequential, specific · 1: present but generic · 0: no clear stakes |
| **Escalation** | 0–2 | 2: episode ends demonstrably higher than it began · 1: modest · 0: flat or lower |
| **Emotional Investment** | 0–2 | 2: strong attachment to at least one character · 1: mild · 0: no reason to care |
| **Cliffhanger** | 0–1 | 1: specific, compelling, unresolvable hook · 0: weak, fake, or absent |

**Interpretation:** 9–10 excellent, proceed · 8 strong, proceed · 7 acceptable, review weakest category first · 5–6 revise before production · 0–4 full brief revision.

**Standalones:** no Cliffhanger category; max 9, target 7+. A standalone below 6 should be revised or not produced.

**Roles:** Hal provides the initial NEDS score with each brief · Orion reviews any brief at 7 or below and escalates 6-or-below to Marc · Marc is final authority on disputes and any production approval below 7 · Vega reviews finished audio against the Emotional Investment category and flags failures.

The `NEDS_SCORE:` header field is required on all scripts (format owned by Spec v1.5 / Stage 2 v2.3).

# PART 9 — COMPETENCE RULE

Listeners enjoy watching intelligent people solve difficult problems. Capable protagonists create identification through admiration, not pity.

**Preferred protagonist types:** detectives and investigators · dispatchers and communications operators · engineers and technical specialists · pilots, captains, vehicle operators · entrepreneurs under pressure · journalists and researchers · nurses, paramedics, emergency responders · lawyers and prosecutors · search-and-rescue specialists · scientists and analysts.

**The Competence–Stakes Ratio:** the stronger the competence, the higher the stakes must be. A capable person facing a minor problem produces no tension; a capable person facing a problem that *exceeds* their competence produces maximum tension. *The detective who solves every case must face a case that cannot be solved.* Design the problem to exceed the protagonist's capability — not crush it, exceed it.

**Helpless protagonists** — characters who exist primarily to react, suffer, or be rescued — are not preferred. If a protagonist cannot demonstrate competence in at least one domain, reconsider the characterization. Suffering is not a character trait.

# PART 10 — CHARACTER ATTACHMENT & FRANCHISE EVALUATION

Listeners return for characters more than plots. A great plot can be replaced by the next plot; a great character cannot. After every series finale, ask: **would a listener want another story with this protagonist even if the mystery is completely solved?** (The model: Bosch, Reacher, Holmes, Poirot — the mystery ends, the protagonist continues.)

**Process:** within 5 business days of a series finale entering the catalog, Hal submits a Character Attachment Evaluation to Orion.

**Scoring (0–8):**
| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Character Appeal** | vehicle for plot only | some memorable traits | distinct personality, unresolved interior life |
| **World Depth** | world exists only for this story | partial — some unexplored threads | rich world, multiple secondary characters and locations |
| **Future Story Potential** | arc fully closed | one or two directions possible | multiple natural directions emerge organically |
| **Listener Return Potential** | completion below 70% | completion 70–85% | completion 85%+, clear demand signals |

**Interpretation:** 7–8 recommend franchise continuation to Marc (long-term asset) · 5–6 optional, Marc decides on catalog strategy · 3–4 series complete, consider retiring the protagonist · 0–2 do not continue.

**Continuation rules for franchise characters (score 5+):** new seasons must raise the stakes beyond the previous season, develop the protagonist in a new direction, introduce at least one major new character/threat/location, and receive full series-design approval from Marc before Episode 1.

This framework replaces the v2.0 Franchise Candidate template.

# PART 11 — HIDDEN LESSON RULE

Stories can carry meaning. They should not carry messages. A message is delivered; a meaning is discovered — and a listener who discovers an insight owns it.

Characters must never: explain the theme, summarize what the experience meant, deliver wisdom speeches, or act as philosophical mouthpieces. Characters must: make decisions that reveal character, face consequences that carry meaning, and change in ways the listener recognizes as true.

**The Discovery Test:** if the lesson can be stated in one sentence without reference to any plot event, it is too explicit.

**Deprecated pattern (auto-flag in validation):** final narrator lines beginning *"She had learned that…" / "He understood now that…" / "The truth was that…"*
**The standard:** the final narrator line is a specific, concrete image, action, or observation. The listener draws the conclusion.

---

# PART 12 — BELIEVABILITY RULE

Default to believable. Unless the genre requires otherwise, stories take place in a world that feels real, obeys real-world logic, and involves recognizably human behavior. A distracted driver who catches an implausible detail mentally leaves the story.

| Genre | Standard |
|---|---|
| Thriller, Mystery, Crime, Drama, Western, Adventure | Strict — real-world logic throughout |
| Horror | Believable setup; supernatural introduced gradually |
| Supernatural | Internally consistent rules, established early |
| Sci-Fi | Near-future or believable science preferred |
| Historical Drama | Period-accurate; no anachronistic behavior or values |

**The Commitment Rule:** a genre that permits the unbelievable must be internally consistent. Ghosts are fine; ghosts that follow no discernible rules are not. Inconsistency within a genre is more damaging than genre commitment.

# PART 15 — THE FIRST THREE MINUTES RULE
## Early Investment & Orientation Doctrine

**Added:** 2026-06-26 · Marc Postlewaite
**Authority:** Creative doctrine. Also enforced as a production gate (see ET-COS Book VI, Chapter 10).

### Core Principle

> **Clarity of situation. Mystery of cause.**

We do not explain everything early. But the listener must understand enough to care. The listener should know **what is happening** before being asked to wonder **why it is happening**.

Mystery is welcome. Confusion is not.

### The Five-Question Test

At the three-minute mark of any episode, a first-time listener must be able to answer all five:

1. **Who am I emotionally following?**
2. **What is happening right now?**
3. **What does this person want, fear, or need?**
4. **Why does it matter?**
5. **What question am I listening to answer?**

If a listener cannot answer all five by minute three, the opening has failed.

### Production Requirements

- The protagonist or emotional anchor must be clear within the first **60–90 seconds**.
- The listener must understand the immediate situation within the first **2 minutes**.
- The protagonist must have visible pressure — need, wound, danger, obligation, or desire — early.
- The opening must create **one clear listening question**.
- The opening must create at least one of: sympathy, admiration, urgency, fear, curiosity, concern, or emotional identification.
- **Episode 1 of any series** is held to extra strictness — it establishes listener attachment for the entire series.
- **Every non-final episode** must re-orient the listener quickly before deepening the episode's conflict.
- The story must not rely on "it makes sense later" as the main opening strategy.

### What Is and Is Not Allowed

**Allowed:** Mystery of cause — withheld backstory, unnamed threat, ambiguous antagonist, deferred explanation of why something is happening.

**Not allowed:** Mystery of situation — the listener not knowing who they are following, what is physically happening, or why they should care.

### Repair Protocol

When an opening fails the Five-Question Test:

| Failure | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Mysterious but confusing | Clarify the situation. Preserve the mystery of cause. |
| Plot before emotional attachment | Add personal stakes, pressure, vulnerability, competence, or desire earlier. |
| No clear protagonist in 90 seconds | Move the protagonist's name, action, or feeling into the opening scene. |
| Atmosphere without a person | Anchor the atmosphere to someone experiencing it. |
| Listening question is vague | Make the question concrete and felt. |

**The fix is never "explain more." The fix is "anchor the listener earlier."**

# PART 16 — MINIMUM CHARACTER AGE RULE (MINOR-VOICE-001)

**No speaking character in any Endless Tales story or series may be under 14 years of age.**

This is a hard gate enforced at brief stage. It is not a preference or a style guideline.

## The Rule

Children under 14 may exist in a story — as plot elements, referenced family members, background figures — but they may not deliver spoken dialogue. Their words, if story-relevant, are reported by the narrator in indirect speech:

> ✅ ALLOWED: *She told him she wasn't coming. Her voice had gone flat, the way it did when she had already decided.*
> ❌ NOT ALLOWED: `SOPHIE: I'm not coming. I've already decided.` — spoken dialogue assigned to an 8-year-old character.

The line is drawn at quoted, performable dialogue — a line a voice actor would record. Narrative description of a child's actions, words, or emotions is fine and is not restricted by this rule.

## Why 14

The age ceiling of 14 is set deliberately above the threshold where a voice is unambiguously adult. A 15- or 16-year-old voice sits in a register that is often indistinguishable from a young adult. A 12- or 13-year-old voice is not. The ceiling prevents a production request for a child-sounding voice from being technically compliant by assigning a character age of 13. See compliance document MINOR-VOICE-001 (docs/compliance/) for the full legal and platform-policy rationale.

## Approved Alternatives

If a story requires a child character as a significant presence:

1. **Indirect speech** — narrator reports the child's words. Best option; often more evocative than direct dialogue.
2. **Raise the character's age to 14+** — explicit author instruction required; must be narratively plausible.
3. **Non-speaking role** — child is present and affects the plot but does not speak on-record.
4. **Adult voice, directed younger** — an adult performer cast to read the lines; acceptable only if the character is 14 or above.

## Enforcement

This rule is enforced at **brief stage**, before a script is drafted:
- Any `CHARACTERS:` entry with a stated age below 14 who has dialogue → brief rejected
- Any `CHARACTERS:` entry without a stated age for a speaking character → brief rejected (age is required; see STORY_BRIEF_TEMPLATE v2.3)
- Hal does not silently age-up a character to pass the gate. Age changes require explicit written instruction from Marc or Atlas and must be logged.

This rule is enforced a second time at **script stage** in the SCRIPT VALIDATOR (v1.4, Section 2C).

### Agent Implementation Duties (Part 16 additions)

**Claude (story writing):** If a story premise requires a child character with significant dialogue, propose a narrative workaround in the brief response before drafting. Do not draft dialogue for characters under 14. If a brief is received with such a character, return a gate rejection with a specific workaround suggestion.

**Hal (production oversight):** Add the Part 16 check to every brief review. A brief failing this gate does not proceed to Stage 2 under any circumstance.

**Orion (operations):** Monitor for any validator failure under Section 2C. Two or more occurrences in a calendar month → escalate to Marc as a systemic brief-quality issue.
