[START AUDIO DRAMA SCRIPT]

MARA: Lena didn't explain where we were going. She just drove.

[SFX: Car accelerating hard into city traffic — night, tires on wet pavement, a sharp left turn]

MARA: I watched the Palmetto Hotel drop behind us through the rear window. The Unknown Man still inside it, sitting in his chair the way he'd sat since before we arrived — the way someone sits when they have been carrying something for thirty years and have finally found the specific person to hand it to.

MARA: But we didn't have thirty years. Lena had said move. So we moved.

JUNE BELL: Where are we going?

LENA: Liberty Bridge. Adrian's body was still under the bridge when I left. I left Officer Santos with it and told him to call nothing in and touch nothing. Santos hasn't answered two radio calls.

MARA: Someone sedated him.

LENA: That's what I'm driving toward to find out.

[BEAT]

MARA: I looked at Eli. He was holding the carbon copy form he'd taken from the cassette recorder's hiding spot — one sheet of onionskin paper, the kind of duplicate made by an old typewriter with a carbon sheet between pages. He was holding it the way you hold something that might dissolve if you press too hard.

MARA: What does it say?

ELI: Property easement. 1997. Subsurface access rights to the tunnel infrastructure beneath Palmetto Hotel, Block C — Reedy River Corridor. Signed by the city's Chief Infrastructure Officer at the time.

MARA: Who?

ELI: Gerald Patton. He's been dead since 2004.

LENA: And the second signature?

MARA: I leaned forward. There was a second line at the bottom of the form — a countersignature line, partially smeared in the carbon transfer.

ELI: I can't read the name. The ink transferred unevenly on the carbon. I can see a first initial — I think it's a D — and then the surname begins with a V. The rest is gone.

[BEAT]

MARA: D.V.

---

MARA: Santos was on the ground under Liberty Bridge when we got there. Not dead. His pulse was regular, his breathing was fine — the particular quality of someone who had been put down chemically and was working his way back up from the bottom of it.

[SFX: Running footsteps on the concrete embankment beneath a bridge — water close, traffic above, a man groaning]

LENA: Santos. Can you hear me?

OFFICER SANTOS: I — I couldn't —

LENA: Stay down. Look at the light.

OFFICER SANTOS: There was a woman. She had — I thought she was just walking, just walking past, and then I couldn't —

MARA: The body was gone. The place where Adrian Cross had lain — pressed against the embankment wall, eyes open, papery and still — was empty. Clean. Too clean. Someone had not just moved him; they'd given the concrete a reason not to remember him.

LENA: How long ago?

OFFICER SANTOS: I don't — maybe forty minutes. Maybe.

MARA: Forty minutes. We had been in the Palmetto Hotel with the Unknown Man and June Bell for longer than that.

MARA: Someone had known exactly when to come.

[BEAT]

LENA: Was he carrying anything? On his person — a token, a disc, about this size.

OFFICER SANTOS: I didn't search him. You told me not to touch anything.

LENA: I know. I'm asking what you observed.

OFFICER SANTOS: His left hand. It was closed. When I — I checked his pulse at the wrist, his left hand was closed around something.

MARA: Now it wasn't.

---

MARA: I had been carrying Adrian's token in my jacket pocket since the tunnel — the one he'd pressed into my hand with the last of whatever was holding him together. That token was still with me. The person who took Adrian's body had not taken mine.

MARA: But Adrian had come to that tunnel with something. Whatever was in his closed left hand — whatever he'd held onto past the point of holding onto anything else — it was gone now.

LENA: There's a second token. There was always a second token.

ELI: He would have had the next one on the route. That's how the system works — each checkpoint caretaker carries the token to the next station. If Adrian was the first caretaker Mara was supposed to find, he would have had token two.

MARA: Someone took token two off a dead man's hand in the forty minutes we were in a basement talking.

ELI: Someone who knew Adrian would be there. And knew what he'd be carrying.

[BEAT]

JUNE BELL: I know where checkpoint four is.

MARA: She said it the way people say something they've been saving. Not performing it. Just releasing it — the way you set down something heavy in the one place it was always going to land.

MARA: How long have you known that?

JUNE BELL: Since 1997. Since before your mother disappeared.

---

[SFX: A warehouse door sliding on a metal track — the sound of a space that has been closed for years, air escaping in one long exhale]

MARA: June drove. She knew the way without looking at anything except the road — the specific navigation of someone who has been to a place enough times in their memory that the physical route is just confirmation.

MARA: She took us east of the Palmetto Hotel, past the old textile district, to a half-block of buildings the city had designated for review and never reviewed. The building she stopped at had been a cotton factor's warehouse in the 1890s. It had been other things since then. In 1997, according to no public record, it had been checkpoint four on a flood-watch route that the city pretended had ended decades earlier.

JUNE BELL: In there. Past the loading bay, there's a staircase that goes down.

LENA: How far?

JUNE BELL: One level. The chamber is behind a green door at the bottom. It won't be locked — it wasn't built to be locked from inside.

MARA: Have you been here recently?

JUNE BELL: Not in twenty-nine years.

MARA: Someone has.

JUNE BELL: I know.

[SFX: Concrete steps descending, footsteps echoing differently as the ceiling lowers — the sound of below-ground space opening ahead]

MARA: The green door was steel, painted over decades ago and touched again recently — I could see where someone had gripped the edge and the paint had come away in a clean arc, the mark of a hand used to opening this particular door.

MARA: Inside: a chamber roughly the size of a shipping container, with a floor that sloped slightly toward a drain at the center. Old wooden shelving along one wall. A lantern — battery-powered, current model, half-drained. Beside it, a folding camp stool. And on the stool, sitting in the center of the room as if waiting for someone to notice it properly, a brass token.

MARA: I picked it up.

MARA: The clapper was upright. Right-side up. Not inverted.

[BEAT]

ELI: That's not a worker's token.

MARA: What is it?

ELI: In the original flood-watch system, there were two kinds of token. The inverted clapper marked something found at a checkpoint — evidence, anomaly, something that shouldn't be there. But the upright token was different. It was the administrator's token. The record-keeper. The person who didn't walk the route — who stayed at the center and received the report from whoever came back.

MARA: The person who knows what everyone found at every checkpoint.

ELI: And who never had to go into the tunnels at all.

LENA: Because someone came to them.

[BEAT]

MARA: I looked at June Bell.

MARA: She was looking at the token in my hand. Her face was doing the specific thing a face does when it has stopped deciding whether to speak and has decided.

MARA: I waited.

JUNE BELL: My name is June Bell. That has always been true. My name before I married was June Villanueva. In 1997, when the Camperdown flood-watch records were kept by a contracted city employee — I was that employee. The initials in the ledger that no one could identify — the one beside the upright token mark — were not D.V.

[BEAT]

JUNE BELL: They were J.V. The typist who entered the records had a carbon smear on the key for J and mistakenly stamped it as a D. I have known this for twenty-nine years. I came here tonight because your mother asked me to come if you ever asked. She asked me that in 1994. Three years before she disappeared. She knew something would eventually go wrong.

MARA: She trusted you.

JUNE BELL: She trusted me enough to tell me what she found at checkpoint four and what it meant. And she trusted me enough to tell you, in person, when you were ready to hear it. I believe you are ready now.

[BEAT]

JUNE BELL: What your mother found in 1997 — what she was going to take to the authorities before someone stopped her — was not just a buried record. It was a name. The name of the person who used the flood-watch infrastructure to conceal three bodies in 1997. And that person is still alive. Still in Greenville. And I can prove it.

[SFX: A phone vibrating on concrete — sharp, insistent, wrong]

LENA: That's my contact. The one person in the department I trust. She doesn't call unless something has changed.

LENA: It's changed.

BELLE B: June has been keeping the center of this for twenty-nine years. Next episode — what Claire found at checkpoint four, and the name that was worth a life to keep buried.
