MARA VANCE — 35–38, female, Greenville SC accent, narrator and protagonist. She has her mother's words. Now she has to go to the place her mother described and see what is actually there.
DETECTIVE LENA ORTIZ — mid-40s, female, South Carolina. Moving with the specific efficiency of someone who has accepted that this will become a formal investigation and is already thinking two steps ahead of what that requires.
ELI MERCER — mid-40s, male, Southern academic. His usefulness has shifted — from historical knowledge to physical knowledge of the tunnel maps; he knows the infrastructure well enough to navigate, which means he has been thinking about this for longer than he has admitted.
UNKNOWN MAN — calm, Southern, deliberate. Has been in the passage below the Palmetto Hotel for months. He is not a ghost. He is a flood-watch worker who signed in on June 18, 1997, and never signed out, because signing out would have required admitting he was alive when two other men were not.

[START AUDIO DRAMA SCRIPT]

MARA: My mother's affidavit described the seventh checkpoint in a way that only made sense if you had stood at checkpoint four and knew which direction further meant.

MARA: We went back to the warehouse. June stayed with Marisol — the affidavit needed to stay with a witness who could testify to the chain of custody, and June's testimony was going to be needed regardless of what we found. They had calls to make that I was glad not to have to make.

MARA: The three of us went back underground.

[SFX: The warehouse staircase again — footsteps descending concrete, the shift in acoustics as the ceiling lowers]

MARA: The passage Claire had described was not on the map Eli had memorized from the Camperdown archives. That was the point. It had been built — or rather, had been incorporated — in the original flood-watch construction and then deliberately omitted from every subsequent document. Not sealed. Not destroyed. Just unmapped. Rooms only stay secret when someone needs them to.

ELI: From checkpoint four, she writes north-northeast, past the drainage partition, through a section she describes as "the place where the stone floor changes color from gray to brown." She walked it in October of 1997. She measured it in paces.

LENA: How many?

ELI: Forty-one. Then a door she describes as newer than the infrastructure around it — installed deliberately, at some point between the original construction and 1997.

MARA: And on the other side of the door?

ELI: She doesn't describe what she sees first. She describes what she hears first.

[BEAT]

MARA: We found the partition. We found the section where the floor changed — not a dramatic shift, just a gradual darkening of the stone from the particular gray of Greenville's river-quarried construction to a deeper brown, the color of iron in water. Forty-one of my mother's paces put us at a door that was, as she had written, newer than everything around it.

MARA: Steel. A handle rather than a lock. Someone had designed this to open from either side.

[SFX: A steel door handle being pressed — not locked; the door swinging inward, releasing a pocket of different air — older, drier, held]

MARA: The air that came out when we opened it was the air of a space that had been sealed and then unsealed and then maintained — not the static air of something forgotten, but the air of something tended.

MARA: Someone had been here recently enough that the air still carried the particular warmth of a human body.

LENA: Hands where I can see them. Now.

MARA: He was in the corner, sitting the same way he had been sitting in the Palmetto Hotel basement. Not hiding. Not crouching. The specific stillness of someone who had already decided what they were going to say when this moment arrived, and had been waiting for it to arrive.

UNKNOWN MAN: I wondered if you'd come back for me or go on ahead. I decided you'd go on ahead. I was right.

MARA: The Unknown Man.

UNKNOWN MAN: I had a key to the hotel. I've had it for twenty-two months. I have been using this space because it is one of the few places in Greenville where Arthur Brenn's people cannot see me without actually entering it.

LENA: And you couldn't just — tell someone.

UNKNOWN MAN: Tell who. The detective who would have had to file a report that goes to her supervisor who reports to someone who eventually reports to the Basin Restoration Committee? I have been waiting for the right specific set of people to find their way here through their own means. If I had guided you, anything I showed you would have my fingerprints on it. This way you've come through the keyhole your mother built and I am one more piece of what you found.

[BEAT]

MARA: What is your name?

UNKNOWN MAN: My name is Victor Dallen. In 1997, I was twenty-two years old and I worked flood-watch checkpoints three through five for the city. On June 18, 1997, I signed into checkpoint three at 11:42 PM with two other men — Thomas Erady and Luis Sandoval. Thomas and Luis did not come back from checkpoint five.

[BEAT]

UNKNOWN MAN: I came back from checkpoint five.

UNKNOWN MAN: I have known, for twenty-nine years, why they didn't.

---

MARA: He told it without drama. The particular flatness of a story that has been held for so long it has worn smooth — no rough edges left, just the shape of what happened.

UNKNOWN MAN: Checkpoint five was below the Palmetto Hotel. That was the end of the route we were assigned. Thomas and Luis went in first. I heard two sounds — one I understood and one I didn't. Then nothing. I went in. What I found — what I understood I had found — was that Thomas and Luis had been killed by someone who had been waiting past checkpoint five — in the chamber through the passage at the end of the route. Someone who knew we would come.

UNKNOWN MAN: I was twenty-two. I ran. I understood, running, that if whoever killed them knew I had survived, I would not survive for long. I never went home. I never filed anything. I understood that someone in the city infrastructure had known we would be there that night, at that time, and had arranged to make sure we didn't leave.

MARA: Arthur Brenn.

UNKNOWN MAN: I didn't know his name until four years ago. Until I saw his photograph in a news article about the Basin Restoration Committee and understood that he had built his career toward a position that would eventually give him authority over this specific corridor.

UNKNOWN MAN: He has an application pending with the county to access this section for a "structural assessment." The application was filed three weeks ago.

LENA: Three weeks.

UNKNOWN MAN: I came back to this space three weeks ago because I realized he was coming. And then you arrived, which was — not what I expected. But better.

---

[SFX: A low resonant vibration — deep, structural, felt more than heard — running through the stone floor and the walls simultaneously]

MARA: We all felt it at the same time. Not a sound, exactly. More like the stone remembering something.

MARA: Then, once — clear, deep, the sound of something large and old and made of metal being set in motion by a force that had nothing to do with any of us:

[SFX: A single bell tone — deep, resonant, felt in the chest — one strike, then the long fade of old brass vibrating in a sealed space]

MARA: The bell.

MARA: It wasn't ringing because someone had rung it. It was ringing because the passage above it — the basin restoration excavation, three weeks of test drilling on the surface — had sent a vibration through the stone, and whatever held the bell in place had finally reached the frequency that set it moving.

MARA: Twenty-nine years of being beneath Falls Park, in the dark, perfectly still.

MARA: And then, once: a note.

LENA: Where is it coming from?

UNKNOWN MAN: Through that passage. Further in.

MARA: I looked at the passage he indicated. The stone beyond it was different — older, pre-mill, the construction of people who built for permanence in an era that understood permanence as something you put into the earth.

MARA: Thomas Erady and Luis Sandoval had gone through that passage twenty-nine years ago and not come back.

MARA: I looked at Lena.

MARA: She was already moving.

BELLE B: The bell rang once. What's on the other side of that passage is what Arthur Brenn has spent twenty-nine years trying to keep buried. Next episode — Mara goes through.
