[START AUDIO DRAMA SCRIPT]

BELLE: Three women vanished from Greenville, South Carolina — and someone arranged it so only one journalist would ever find out why.

NARRATOR: I covered three disappearances in Greenville and got every public fact right. I had the whole truth wrong.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: I keep the photo on my work phone. Still, now. I've never printed it. Never forwarded it. Some images you protect from yourself — from the impulse to open them at odd hours and pretend you understood what you were seeing when you first looked. I didn't understand it. Not that morning under Liberty Bridge, standing in the river mist watching a diver hand it to a detective. Not for a long while after.

[SFX: Early morning river ambient — water under Liberty Bridge, light wind, distant traffic on the bridge above]

NARRATOR: My name is Claire Bennett. I've been covering crime for WGVL News Radio for twelve years. I've done the two-in-the-morning crime scenes, the victim impact statements read on-air without my voice breaking, the door knocks three hours after a shooting where I ask people to describe someone who won't ever come home. I'm not someone who hesitates. And I'm not someone who gets afraid of a phone call.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: May eighth. Thursday. The tip came through the station's general line at five-forty-eight a.m. Anonymous caller, male, forty-two seconds. A salvage diver found a case under Liberty Bridge. That's all. The scanner confirmed it seven minutes later, and I was already on Main Street when it came through. I was on scene in eleven minutes.

[SFX: Car door — creak and slam — gravel underfoot on a riverbank, water sound closer now]

NARRATOR: The Reedy River runs low in May. The water level below Liberty Bridge drops enough that you can see the old diagonal ironwork on the lower concrete supports — the ones that go down into the mud. Pete Larkin, salvage diver, did most of his work on insurance finds. He came up holding a sealed waterproof travel case. Black. About the size of a carry-on. The kind you can buy for two hundred dollars at any outdoor retailer. Perfectly sealed. No water intrusion.

[SFX: Diver surfacing — water sounds — voices murmuring in the distance]

CLAIRE: Detective Reyes — can I get eyes on it before it's bagged?

NARRATOR: Detective Ana Reyes had worked the Osei disappearance from week one. She looked at me for four full seconds. The kind of look that's a calculation, not a consideration. Then she nodded.

[SFX: Evidence tent — zipper, tarp laid flat, plastic shuffling]

NARRATOR: Three items, laid on a blue tarp in the white light of a work lamp. First: a laminated city planning map. Greenville municipality. Reedy River corridor, full basin. Stamped across the top in red: Proposed Development Overlay — Revision 7. A document I recognized — I'd requested a copy four months earlier as part of a routine council coverage story and been told it wasn't final yet. Second: a spiral-bound trail log from Falls Park. Handwritten, two different hands — one careful and precise, one looser, more hurried. Dated entries running across eight weeks. The last entry, three lines into a page, stopped mid-sentence. Third: a camera memory card in a static-protection sleeve. Sealed in its own plastic evidence bag. Diane Cho's name written on the sleeve in black marker — her own handwriting. I knew it because I'd read her signed statement three weeks earlier.

[SFX: Paper handling — slight rustle]

NARRATOR: And the phone. A prepaid burner. Black, no distinguishing marks. Detective Reyes held it up in a gloved hand. One app visible on the screen — a contacts list with a single entry. No name. Ten digits.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: I took a photo of the screen before she sealed the bag. She didn't see me do it. I'm not going to pretend I'm proud of that. I am going to tell you that it was the right call.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: Three women. I need you to understand who they were before I tell you what came next. Because the official record had treated them as separate cases, separate tragedies, entirely unconnected — and I had helped build that record, one filed story at a time.

[SFX: Quiet office ambient — WGVL radio station, low background hum]

NARRATOR: Renata Osei. City planner. Forty-six years old. Married, two grown kids, fifteen years with the Greenville municipal planning office. She left City Hall on a Tuesday evening — badge swipe logged at eight-fourteen PM. Her car sat in the municipal parking garage for two days before a colleague noticed. Keys in the ignition. Phone in the cup holder. Handbag on the passenger seat. No sign of a struggle. No witnesses. Renata: gone.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: Mara Fields. Falls Park ranger. Thirty-eight. She'd been with the park service for nine years, knew every trail in the park by feel. Her ranger truck was found at the Falls overlook when her relief came on shift at six-fifteen a.m. Radio still on. Her personal gear — pack, water, weatherproof jacket — all in the truck. The park had no record of a call-out that night. No note. No word to anyone. Mara: gone.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: Diane Cho. Freelance photographer. Thirty-one. She'd been building a name for herself — nature photography, some commercial work, a magazine spread in the works. Her studio on North Main was unlocked when her assistant arrived the morning after she disappeared. A half-finished cup of coffee still on the worktable. Her camera on its tripod, set up mid-shoot. Her phone last pinged a cell tower two blocks from Greenville's old train terminal, ninety-one days before Thursday morning. Then: nothing.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: Three women. Three open cases. Three different neighborhoods, three different employers, three different circles of friends. Nothing in the official record that linked them — and I had written all three stories dutifully, carefully, and completely wrong. Not false — never false. But wrong about what mattered.

[SFX: Rain beginning — car interior, engine off, windows fogging slightly]

NARRATOR: I sat in my car at the Liberty Bridge scene for twenty-two minutes before I called the number. I know because I watched the clock and made myself admit, for the first time in twelve years, that I was afraid to dial a phone.

[SFX: Phone dialing — deliberate, slow — then ringing, once, twice]

NARRATOR: On the second ring, before a voice could answer: a text notification. Anonymous relay service. Three characters: 2-1-4.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: Locker 214. There was only one place in Greenville where that made immediate sense.

[SFX: Rain on windshield — car starting — driving]

NARRATOR: The old train terminal on West Washington Street. The passenger lines had been mostly decommissioned for years — some freight still moved through the yard, but the main concourse was half-leased to a rotating set of small vendors. Antique seller in the south corner, a café that kept inconsistent hours. Concrete floors. High ceilings. The old brass locker banks along the north wall, platform-level, still in use for storage and the occasional traveler. I'd been inside maybe a dozen times.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: I've never been bothered by the terminal. Some people find it unsettling — the way the fluorescent lights flicker even when they're functioning correctly, the way sound behaves in the empty concourse, pooling and echoing where it shouldn't. The smell, still faintly of coal and old paper despite decades without steam engines. I'd always found it just atmospheric. Evidence of time passing. Harmless.

[SFX: Car parking — engine off — footsteps on old concrete, slightly hollow]

NARRATOR: I went in at eight fifty-seven a.m. The antique seller wasn't in yet. The concourse was empty except for the ambient light through the upper windows and the flicker of the fluorescent tubes along the wall. The locker bank ran the full length of the north side — brass-faced, numbered in old black metal stampings. 214 was three-quarters down, at about chest height.

[SFX: Footsteps slowing — locker bank ambient — very slight sound of distant HVAC]

NARRATOR: The key fob on the burner phone's lanyard. I'd assumed it was for the suitcase itself when I first saw it. I'd been wrong. I held it against the locker face and the LED strip above the lock went green on the first touch.

[SFX: LED beep — soft — metallic click — locker swinging open slowly, a slight creak]

NARRATOR: Inside: one pair of over-ear headphones. Black. Consumer model — nothing special, a brand you'd find at any electronics store. The battery indicator on the left cup read full. The cord was wound neatly, secured with a cable tie. A strip of white tape on the left cup had three words printed on it in the same black marker as Diane Cho's memory card sleeve.

[BEAT]

CLAIRE: Put them on.

[PAUSE:2]

NARRATOR: I stood in the terminal for — I don't know exactly. Thirty seconds. Maybe a minute. Holding a pair of headphones that someone had placed in a locker that only a burner phone pulled from the bottom of the Reedy River could open, at the end of a chain that started with a suitcase deliberately placed against a bridge support. Placed for a diver to find. Placed for me to find. Someone knew I would be on scene. Someone knew I would photograph the contact number. Someone knew I would make the call.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: Someone had been watching me. Or they'd known how I worked, which amounts to the same thing.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: I put the headphones on.

[SFX: Headphone cushions settling — slight pressure — a breath of white noise, like the gap between recording tracks]

NARRATOR: The first few seconds were silence. Not the absence of sound — something denser. The hiss you hear between spoken parts on an old tape, the silence that still holds the shape of what was said. I stood there with the headphones on and heard nothing but that quiet, and I had time to feel — briefly, completely — like I had made a serious mistake.

[SFX: Fluorescent lights — far end of the terminal — a low stutter, one tube going]

NARRATOR: Then the lights started.

[SFX: Fluorescent flicker — spreading section by section, traveling down the north wall — then a deeper hum as the main overhead banks begin to go]

NARRATOR: Not all at once. It moved. Starting at the far south end of the concourse and traveling toward me, section by section, like someone walking a switch down the wall. Each bank of lights flickering and then dying. One, then the next, then the next. I watched it come toward me and I didn't move. I couldn't have told you why.

[SFX: Final overhead bank — a longer flicker — then out. Full darkness. No ambient light.]

NARRATOR: The entire terminal floor went dark. Not a circuit breaker trip — that's a pop, a single moment. This was deliberate. Rolling. Controlled.

[BEAT]

NARRATOR: I couldn't see the locker in front of me. I couldn't see the exit door twenty feet to my right. I stood in the dark with someone else's headphones on my ears and waited.
