# Wearing My Face
**Mystery/Thriller · Greenville, SC · Cass Boone (first-person)**

---

## EPISODE 1 — ~90 sec · 260 words

She's wearing my face. Right here in Greenville, under Liberty Bridge — the dead woman in the river is wearing my face.

I keep telling myself it's a trick of the light. The sodium lamps on the bridge, the way water blurs a face. But Detective Ward turns her toward me, and I see the scar above her left eyebrow — the one I got at nine years old falling off my grandmother's porch — and my knees hit the concrete.

"Ma'am." His hand finds my shoulder. "Do you know this woman?"

I can't answer. How do I answer that?

She has my nose. My gap-toothed smile. The mole just below my right ear that I've spent thirty years forgetting is there. She's me, and she's dead, and she was pulled from the Reedy River at 11:47 PM on a Tuesday, and I am standing right here, alive, not knowing what alive means anymore.

"I don't know her," I say. Because what else do I say? I don't know what I'm looking at.

Ward writes in his notebook. Doesn't look up.

"We found your ID on her," he says. "Your wallet. Your phone. Your keys."

He finally meets my eyes.

"So help me understand, Ms. Boone — if she's not you..."

He leaves it open. He wants me to finish the sentence.

I can't.

Because the question underneath his question — the one neither of us is saying — is the one that just cracked open the floor of everything I thought I knew about myself.

*Who is she wearing?*

## EPISODE 2 — ~3 min · 539 words

He takes my fingerprints and hers, right there on the bank in Greenville, and they match.

He doesn't say it. He closes the card and looks at the Reedy River and says, "We'll need the lab to confirm." But I watch his face when he holds the two prints side by side, and something shifts behind his eyes — a microexpression of pure, controlled panic, and Ward is not a man who panics easily.

"Detective," I say. "Are they the same?"

"We'll need the lab," he says again. That's not a no.

They keep me there another hour. Questions I can't answer. Who would want to do this? Do I have enemies? Have I noticed anything unusual — anyone following me, anyone too interested? I say no to all of it, because the honest answer is that my life is so ordinary it barely leaves a mark. I'm thirty-two years old. I do data entry for a logistics company in downtown Greenville. I don't have enemies. I have a dentist appointment next Thursday and a half-finished puzzle on my coffee table and a cat I need to feed.

The cat. I'd completely forgotten about Theo.

I drive home on autopilot. My apartment is twelve minutes from Liberty Bridge — east side, second floor of a converted Victorian on Rutherford Road. Four years of my life in those walls. I know every creak in the floor, every trick of light at 2 AM, which cabinet sticks when the humidity climbs. This place knows me back.

I open the door and reach for the light switch. The lamp comes on. Everything is where I left it.

Except.

On my kitchen table — clean when I left at nine PM, never left cluttered — there is a coffee cup. The ceramic one with the chip in the handle I've been meaning to replace since March.

It's still warm.

Theo is watching me from the top of the refrigerator. He doesn't come down. He only does that when strangers have been in the apartment.

Someone was here. Someone who knows which cup I use, who knows how to get in, who was sitting in my kitchen while I was standing on a riverbank looking at my own dead face. I stand very still and listen. The apartment sounds empty. But it felt this empty before, didn't it? And it wasn't.

I pick up my phone to call Ward.

One ring.

"I was wondering when you'd find her."

My voice. My exact voice — the low pitch I hate in recordings, the way I flatten my vowels, the slight catch at the back of my throat when I'm thinking. Coming from the other end of the line.

"Don't call Ward again, Cass." A pause. "We have a lot to talk about."

The call ends.

I stand in my kitchen with a warm cup and a dead phone and the slow, terrible understanding that whoever has my face also has my voice, my name, and my phone number memorized. They knew I would call. They knew exactly what I would do.

They've been watching me long enough to know me better than I know myself.

And they're still out there. Somewhere in Greenville. Wearing me.

## EPISODE 3 — ~5 min · 727 words

I redial. Of course I redial — I'm standing in my kitchen in Greenville at midnight holding a phone that just spoke in my voice, and I redial.

The number that called me is my own number. The phone in my hand. It rings four times and goes to voicemail and I hear myself say to leave a message at the tone, and I hang up without speaking. What do you say to yourself?

I sit on the kitchen floor because my legs won't hold me and the chair feels too far away. Theo eventually comes down from the refrigerator and puts himself in my lap, which is the most useful thing anyone does for me that night.

I call Ward at 2:17 AM.

"The fingerprints," I say when he answers. "Tell me."

Silence. Then: "Ms. Boone, you should get some sleep."

"They matched."

Another silence. Longer.

"They were close," he says finally. "Closer than they should be. We're running the DNA."

"Ward."

"Get some sleep." He hangs up.

Close. Closer than they should be. Which means he doesn't understand it either. Which means it's not a sophisticated mask, not a coincidence. Something real happened to make a dead woman's hands look exactly like mine.

I don't sleep. I go through my apartment instead — not searching, exactly, more like inventorying. Making sure I still exist. This is my coffee cup. This is my cat. These are my things. I am real.

At 4 AM, I find something that isn't mine.

In the top drawer of my desk — beneath the takeout menus and dead batteries and the lease I keep forgetting to file — there's a photograph I don't own.

A woman with my face, sitting in a kitchen that isn't mine, at a table with a man I don't recognize. She's laughing. He's looking at her like she's the only thing in the room. They both look comfortable. At home.

On the back, in handwriting that's almost mine but not quite: *Cass and Theo, 2019.*

2019. Two years before I moved to Greenville.

I stare at the man in the photograph. I have never seen his face. And yet I named my cat Theo. The name came to me the day I got him — felt right, felt familiar. I never questioned it. I filed it under instinct and moved on.

I put the photograph back. I close the drawer. And I try, standing in my living room at 4 AM, to remember where I was before Greenville. The memories are there — they're all there, a whole life's worth, childhood and school and a string of apartments — but they feel like photographs too. Things I've seen rather than things I've lived. Smooth around the edges. Neatly arranged. Like someone filed them there for me to find.

Ward comes by at eight. He looks like he hasn't slept either. He has two coffees, hands me one without asking, and says: "Her DNA came back."

"And?"

"She's not a clone. She's not your twin." He turns his cup in his hands. "The lab says she's genetically you. But with markers that shouldn't be possible. Like someone took your genome and—" He stops. Starts again. "Like your DNA was edited. Years ago. And then used to build someone new."

The room goes quiet.

"That's not possible," I say.

"No," Ward says. "It isn't."

We sit with that for a moment.

"There's something else." He sets down his coffee. He pulls out his phone and turns it toward me. A missing persons report — active, filed three days ago. The photograph on the file is my face.

The name on the report is not Cass Boone.

"We got a match this morning," he says. "A family in Spartanburg. Their daughter, missing eight years. Her name was Cassidy Reyes." He pauses. "They provided documentation, Ms. Boone. Birth certificates. School photos. Medical records going back to infancy."

He turns the phone so I can read the last item in the file. A genetic analysis. Filed by a private clinic in 2011. At the bottom: a note in red.

*Donor profile: active. Tissue recovery: pending.*

"Ms. Boone," Ward says quietly. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to answer me honestly."

He sets the phone on the table between us.

"Do you know how you were made?"

## EPISODE 4 — ~13.9 min · 2,784 words
*First full episode. First post-signup episode under Test A.*

"Do you know how you were made?" Ward asks me in my Greenville kitchen, and the correct answer — the only honest answer I have — is no.

Not in the way he means. Not in the clinical, document-verified, birth-certificate-authenticated way he means. I know the story I was told. I know the photographs. I know the particular quality of my mother's voice when she talked about the hospital, the rain that day, my father's hands shaking when he cut the cord. I know these things the way I know the feel of a book I read in childhood — completely, intimately, down to the smell of the pages — and still couldn't tell you if a single word of it is true.

"I was born in Knoxville," I say. "Knox County General. December 12th, 1993."

Ward looks at the file in his lap. He says nothing.

"My mother's name is Patricia Boone. My father was James Boone, deceased 2018. I have a dental record, a social security number, a driving record with two speeding tickets and a parking citation I'm still embarrassed about."

"Cassidy Reyes," Ward says, "was also born in December 1993. In a private clinic outside Spartanburg. The clinic is registered under a shell company. The shell company traces back to a biotech firm that dissolved in 2004." He sets the file on the table between us. "The Reyes family has documentation. Her DNA matches the Reyes family's biological markers. It also matches yours."

"That's not possible. You can't match two different families."

"No," Ward says. "You can't. Which is why the lab ran it four times." He opens the file. "What they found is that your DNA and Cassidy Reyes's DNA share a common template. Not a parent, not a sibling. A template. Like two documents printed from the same file, with different edits applied after the fact."

The room is very quiet. Outside, on Rutherford Road, a delivery truck passes. A dog barks twice and stops.

I have an old scar on my left knee from a bicycle accident I remember clearly — I was eleven, the sidewalk on Maplewood Drive, my friend Julia screaming, the particular green of the grass on the Hendersons' lawn across the street. The smell of cut grass and blood. I can see the exact color of the sky.

That memory feels real.

Every memory feels real.

That is not evidence of anything.

"The photograph I found," I say. "The one in my desk drawer. Of a woman with my face with a man named Theo."

"Tell me about the photograph."

I tell him everything: the kitchen that wasn't mine, the man I didn't recognize, the handwriting on the back that was almost-but-not-quite mine. The fact that I named my cat Theo for reasons I never examined. Ward writes all of it in his notebook. He doesn't react, which I've come to understand means he's reacting a great deal on the inside.

"2019," he says. "Two years before you moved to Greenville."

"I know."

"What do you remember from 2019? Specifically."

"Charlotte. I had an apartment near Dilworth. A job at a market research company. I remember the apartment — third floor, exposed brick, a window that faced west and caught the light at six PM in a way I loved." I stop. "But I don't have a single photograph from that time. I lost my phone. I wasn't on social media. I explained this to myself as a hard year, a private period. The explanations always held when I needed them to."

"And now?"

"Now they don't hold."

Ward closes his notebook. He picks up his coffee. He looks at me over the rim of the cup with the particular steadiness of a man who has learned to deliver news carefully.

"There's a local address," he says. "Connected to the same network as the Spartanburg clinic. Not that facility — a different office. Registered in Greenville in 2019. It closed in 2021, but the building's still standing."

He shows me the address. Stone Avenue. I know that building. I have walked past it a hundred times.

I have never thought twice about it.

"Let's go," I say.

---

The building is a converted bungalow between a tax prep office and a dry cleaner — the kind of unremarkable facade that your eye slides right off because there is nothing on it to catch. No sign. Plantation shutters closed over every window. Three deadbolts on the front door, all unlocked, which Ward notes with the kind of professional grimness that means someone left in a hurry.

Inside: empty. Not abandoned-empty, the gradual vacancy of a space no one has cared for. Recently-emptied. You can smell it — the ghost-warmth of occupancy, a trace of coffee and paper and the metallic note of electronics that have been running for years and then weren't. Dust-free squares on the floor where filing cabinets stood. A desk with a water ring. A phone jack in the wall with a cord-end still plugged into it, cut clean through.

On the wall, a calendar. March. A handwritten note on the 14th: *C.B. — final confirmation. Proceed.*

I moved to Greenville on March 7th.

The confirmation, whatever it was, came one week later.

Ward photographs everything. I drift toward the back — a small room that might once have been a kitchen — and find the one thing they left behind. Not by accident. I'm certain of this. It's sitting open on the counter, centered, like it was meant to be found.

A single page in a manila folder. The header: *MERIDIAN RENEWAL PROTOCOL — INTERNAL SUMMARY.*

Clinical language. I read it twice to get past the jargon.

*Subject Profile: CRB-001. Derived template. Stable. Placed, Greenville SC, March 2022.*
*Subject Profile: CRB-002. Derived template. Stable. Placed —* and here the page is torn. The city name is gone.
*Subject Profile: CRB-003. Derived template. Unstable. Recall initiated.*
*Subject Profile: CRB-004. Derived template. Unstable. Recovered — Liberty Bridge, Greenville SC, July 22, 2026.*

July 22nd. Four days ago. The night they pulled a dead woman from the Reedy River.

The dead woman is CRB-004.

Which makes me CRB-001. The first placed template. The one they put in Greenville in March 2022, which is when I moved here, which I thought was my choice — I'd needed a change, I'd visited once years ago and liked it, Greenville felt like the right size. Those reasons felt like mine. They might not be.

CRB-002 is out there in a city whose name someone tore off this page.

CRB-003 was recalled. I don't know what that means and I'm afraid to find out.

I flip to the back of the folder. A photograph, paper-clipped to the inside back cover.

A woman. Not me — not quite. Similar bone structure, the same nose, the same hairline, but with differences I can see now that I'm calibrated to look for them. Wider-set eyes. A longer jaw. Her face arranged differently at rest — less guarded. More present, somehow, like a person who hasn't spent years quietly feeling like something is slightly wrong without being able to name it.

Under the photograph, a typed label: *Original source. Archive only. No contact protocol.*

The original.

She exists. She is the template all of us were derived from. She is supposed to be finished, categorized, filed away — no contact, no protocol, no awareness of what was done with her face and her genome and whatever else they took from her. She's been archived by people who used her as raw material and then decided she didn't need to know.

I look at her face for a long time.

She looks back from whenever this photo was taken, caught mid-motion, squinting slightly into the sun. Unaware she was being documented. She looks like a person who is just living her life, and someone is watching from a distance and writing it down, and she has no idea.

She looks like me.

She looks like she didn't know any of this was happening either.

Ward calls from the front room: "Anything?"

I close the folder. I consider telling him. I consider what he'll do with the information — secure it, process it, run it through systems I don't control. I consider that the woman in that photograph is labeled *no contact protocol*, and if Ward finds out she exists, his protocol might honor that label for reasons that have nothing to do with her safety.

"Nothing useful," I say.

The lie is smooth. It comes easily. I file that away too.

---

Ward drives me home. The rest of the day passes the way bad days do — sluggishly and all at once, the hours both endless and somehow gone. I eat something. I feed Theo. I sit on the couch and stare at the wall while the light moves across the room from east to west and the afternoon becomes evening.

I try to verify myself.

I pull up every record I can find online. Cass Boone. LinkedIn — there I am, data analyst, Greenville SC, connected to people I know. A Facebook account, mostly unused, going back to 2015. Tax records. My credit history. All of it consistent, all of it pointing to a woman who has existed continuously since birth, who attended UNC-Asheville, who has lived in four cities, who is thirty-two years old and currently behind on a library book.

But the earliest digital footprint I have is from 2013. I'm twenty years old in 2013. Before that — nothing findable. Which might mean nothing. Lots of people have thin pre-social-media trails. I've told myself this before.

I also try to call my mother.

The phone rings six times and goes to voicemail. *Patricia Boone, please leave a message.* Her voice, real and familiar, the accent she's never been able to shed from forty years in east Tennessee. I almost leave a message. I almost say: Mom, I need to ask you something about the hospital where I was born, about a clinic, about whether you remember a woman named Cassidy Reyes.

I don't. Because if the memories I have of her are real — if she's real, if she's my actual mother — then this conversation will terrify her. And if she's not — if she's part of whatever infrastructure produced me — then she'll already know everything I might say, and I'll have told them I'm asking questions.

I hang up without speaking.

At 8:43 PM my phone buzzes.

Unknown number. No name.

*You found the file. Good. You're asking the right questions. The woman in the photograph — she wants to meet you. She's ready. Tonight, 10 PM. Northside Storage, Unit 7, off Poinsett Highway, Greenville. Come alone. Do not bring the detective.*

I read it three times. Then I put the phone face-down on the coffee table.

Theo jumps up next to it and looks at me.

I look back at him.

"I know," I say.

---

I leave at nine-thirty. Shoes I can move in. Phone, keys, no pepper spray — I thought about the pepper spray and then thought about using it on a woman who is wearing my face because she was made from the same source I was made from, and I couldn't do it. That might be a mistake. I make it anyway.

I write a note for Theo. This is also a mistake, or at least a species of magical thinking — the idea that leaving a note for my cat will in any way protect me. But I write it: *Back soon. Don't panic. Cass.* I look at my name on the paper and wonder, not for the first time today and probably not for the last, whether it belongs to me.

Northside Storage sits at the north edge of Greenville where Poinsett Highway starts thinning toward Travelers Rest — a flat expanse of corrugated metal units under security lamps that throw the same harsh orange light as the bridge where this started. The parking lot is empty. The facility is silent. A cricket chorus from the grass along the fence line, a car somewhere on the highway behind me. Normal sounds. The world being completely ordinary while mine comes apart.

Unit 7 is near the back corner. The door is down and closed, but there is a sliver of warm light at the bottom.

I park. I sit for a moment with the engine off. I think about Ward. I think about calling him, and I decide I won't, and I examine that decision and I can't fully account for it except to say: whatever is true, I want to hear it first. Before it becomes a police report. Before it becomes a case file. Before someone else decides what it means.

I get out of the car.

The air is warm and thick with late-July humidity, the smell of cut grass and distant rain. Normal. Ordinary. I walk across the parking lot to Unit 7 and I knock twice and I wait.

A latch sounds. The door rolls up four feet and stops.

She ducks under it and stands up straight and looks at me.

She is wearing my face.

Not the dead woman's face — not the slack, drained stillness of someone pulled from a river. She is warm and present and alive, blinking in the orange light. Her eyes are a shade grayer than mine. Her jaw runs a fraction longer. She carries herself with a deliberateness — a stillness that reads as decision rather than habit — that I don't recognize in myself. But the underlying architecture is the same. The nose. The hairline. The way the face arranges itself at rest, the same particular angle of the cheekbones that I've never liked in photographs of myself.

We stand three feet apart and look at each other.

It is the strangest moment of my life, and given what this day has been, that is saying something.

She speaks first.

"Hello, Cassidy," she says. Not Cass. *Cassidy* — the name from the missing persons report, the name on the lab file, a name no one has called me in as long as I can remember, if they ever have at all.

"I'm not going to pretend this isn't hard," she says, quietly. "For either of us. But I need you to know two things before we go any further."

She reaches into her jacket and holds something out to me — a keycard, plain white, a handwritten address and a date two days from now.

"First: I didn't choose any of this either."

I take the card. The address is in a city I don't recognize. The date is Thursday.

"And second." She meets my eyes, and in the orange light her gaze is steadier than mine, steadier than I think I could be right now, and I understand that she has been living with this knowledge for much longer than I have.

"There are more of you," she says. "Not CRB-002. Not three. Eleven total. Most of them don't know what they are. And the people who made you — all of you — are meeting on Thursday to decide what to do with the ones who are still alive."

The crickets keep going. The highway hums.

"That address," she says, nodding at the card in my hand, "is where they're meeting. You can go. Or you can run. But Cassidy —" she holds my gaze without blinking, "— they found CRB-004, and she ran. She ran for eight years."

She doesn't finish the sentence.

She doesn't have to.

I stand in the orange light holding a card with an address I've never seen, looking at my own face wearing an expression I have never felt — the expression of someone who has survived something and is not yet sure what it cost them — and I close my fingers around the card.

"When were you going to tell me?" I say. "Before tonight. When did you know about me?"

She looks at me for a long moment.

"I've known about you since March," she says. "Since the day you moved to Greenville."

The ground does not move. The crickets do not stop. The warm air does not change. And somewhere behind me, I hear the sound of a car entering the parking lot — slow, deliberate, no headlights.

She hears it too. Her eyes move past me to the lot. And for the first time since she stepped out of that storage unit, the steadiness in her face breaks.

"Run," she says.
